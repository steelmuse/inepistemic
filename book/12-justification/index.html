The Unfinished Explainer

XII. Our Justified World

Related material for this chapter

The epistemology explored here thoroughly rejects justification. Knowledge does not become secure through the accumulation of supporting observations. Agreement, confidence, probability, consensus, and coherence are not foundations of truth. Knowledge evolves through problems, conjecture, criticism, error elimination, and the continued survival of explanations that have not yet been eliminated.

Yet ordinary life seems to pull in the opposite direction. We ask for reasons, examples, evidence, confidence, likelihood, and agreement. We teach students to defend theses. We build institutions that reward supported conclusions and statistical tools that assign numerical confidence. We built artificial intelligence systems inside a culture saturated with these justificatory forms.

And when an explanation fits many things we already know, something happens inside you:

it makes sense.

That feeling is not trivial. It can mark successful reconstruction. It can mark explanatory integration. It can mean that things which previously seemed disconnected have become intelligible within one structure.

It can also be mistaken for confirmation.

So the problem is not merely whether justification fails logically. The problem is why it remains so psychologically and culturally natural:

If knowledge does not grow by justification, why does justification feel so natural?

Calling people irrational would explain nothing. It would take the pattern to be explained and turn it into a primitive of human nature.

A better route is to separate two things that usually arrive together in experience. One is the logical structure by which explanations survive criticism. The other is the biological, cultural, and technological machinery through which you actually think. The first can be asymmetric even when the second does not make the asymmetry feel natural.

You can understand general relativity without intuiting curved spacetime. You can read even though your visual system did not evolve for printed language. In the same way, you can understand that matching observations do not justify an explanation. Your mind, culture, and tools can still repeatedly produce the feeling that they do.

An orientation toward disconfirmation may therefore be an achievement. Not because criticism is merely psychologically unpleasant, but because the objective knowledge required to practice it had to be created.

1. How Justification Became Common Sense

The demand for justification is much older than formal epistemology. Long before philosophers asked how knowledge should be grounded, communities already possessed practical structures that authorized belief. Truth could be revealed. A sacred text could disclose it. A priesthood could interpret it. A ruler could embody it. Tradition could preserve it.

Abstract it.

The relation was straightforward:

truth→authority→belief.\text{truth} \rightarrow \text{authority} \rightarrow \text{belief}.truth→authority→belief.

Authority did not merely communicate an explanation. It supplied entitlement to believe.

This matters because justification is not only a technical problem in philosophy. It is part of the architecture by which objective knowledge has historically moved through institutions and into individual minds.

Observation changed that architecture. Instead of asking only:

Who says this is true?

people increasingly asked:

What do we observe?

That change weakened inherited authority and created new forms of criticism. But the demand for epistemic entitlement did not necessarily disappear. It could move from the source of the claim to the observation alleged to support it.

The new question became:

How does observation justify belief?

The authority of revelation could become the authority of observation. The source changed. The justificatory function remained.

The familiar idea that knowledge is justified true belief captures why the function is attractive. You do not want merely to possess a belief. You want it to be true, and you want some relation between the belief and the world that licenses treating it as more than a conjecture.

But finite observations do not entail universal explanatory truth. No number of matching observations can deductively generate the theory that explains them. That is not a practical inconvenience. The entailment is absent.

The anti-justificationist response is therefore not:

certainty would be ideal, but unfortunately we cannot have it.

It is more radical:

justification was never required for knowledge growth in the first place.

You encounter a problem. You create an explanation. You criticize it. You act on it. You replace it when criticism exposes a better problem situation. Nothing in that sequence requires a state called justified belief.

Certainty, however, is not the only form justification can take. Once certainty became difficult to defend, the same function could reappear in weaker language:

  • probably true;
  • highly likely;
  • very high confidence;
  • the weight of the evidence;
  • backed by consensus.

The vocabulary changes from certainty to degrees, while the practical conclusion can remain:

I now possess enough support to treat this explanation as secure.

Probability enters here, but it creates another problem.

Probability is a powerful formal tool. It has uses in science, engineering, finance, communication, logistics, risk management, and many other domains. But formal probability is objective knowledge. It is a cultural invention, recent relative to biological evolution. Whatever biological machinery enables you to create explanations did not evolve by performing probability calculus.

Once learned, however, objective knowledge can become part of the machinery through which you think. If you are trained probabilistically, questions about likelihood, confidence, expected value, and distributions can become cognitively automatic. The tool stops presenting itself as a tool.

Then a dangerous compression becomes possible:

uncertainty is probabilistic.

That sentence sounds more general than the formal structure permits. Probability assigns weight within a represented possibility space. It presupposes outcomes, variables, states, models, or some other structured set over which the distribution is defined.

Show me where that matters.

Take a deck of cards. The deck is finite. The rules are specified. The relevant possibilities are sufficiently closed for calculation.

Now take a scientific anomaly. The theory may not yet exist. The relevant variables may be wrong. The categories may be wrong. Even the problem may be wrong.

The difference is not between precise and imprecise reasoning. It is between assigning weight inside a represented space and creating knowledge that can change or dissolve the represented space itself.

2. When the Problem Changes

The distinction becomes clearest when the evaluative criteria move.

Explain.

Suppose your problem is:

Which job should I take?

You may initially compare salary, prestige, and security. If those criteria stay fixed, the problem can be represented as a choice among known options under a known ranking structure.

Then a different question appears:

Why am I optimizing for prestige?

The available jobs have not changed. What changes is the problem. Autonomy, interest, time, creativity, or relationships may become relevant because the criterion by which the options were being ranked has itself become criticizable.

You have not merely selected a different item from a fixed menu. You have created a different decision problem. This matters because open-ended explanation does not only add possibilities. It can alter what counts as a possibility, what counts as a relevant variable, and what counts as success.

That changes the relation between knowledge and action.

A common defense of justification is practical:

Without confidence, how could you act?

Abstract it.

The question assumes a particular sequence:

assign credence→cross confidence threshold→act.\text{assign credence} \rightarrow \text{cross confidence threshold} \rightarrow \text{act}.assign credence→cross confidence threshold→act.

But action can be organized by a different epistemic structure:

P→TS→A→EE→P2.P \rightarrow TS \rightarrow A \rightarrow EE \rightarrow P_2.P→TS→A→EE→P2​.

You have a problem, PPP. You conjecture a tentative solution, TSTSTS. You act, AAA. The consequences expose errors, EEEEEE. The problem changes into P2P_2P2​.

This sequence does not require final epistemic security. Action is one way a conjecture enters reality strongly enough to encounter criticism.

Newtonian mechanics makes the point concrete. It is not a true account of physical reality, yet it remains useful for enormous classes of problems. Within regimes where relativistic corrections are negligible, Newtonian models can solve the narrower problem well enough to support action.

That does not imply instrumentalism.

What?

The view that ideas and scientific theories are only tools for prediction. Reality still explains why the approximation succeeds where it does. The narrower claim is that truth and local problem adequacy are not identical.

An explanation can therefore be false in general and still be the right problem-solving instrument locally.

Explain.

Practical action does not require the conclusion:

this theory is finally justified.

What matters instead is more specific:

What problem does this model solve, where does it fail, and do those failures matter here?

That question forces a distinction the language of “evidence for a theory” often blurs.

Separate the abstractions.

An explanation asserts dependencies in reality. It says how and why something happens because the structure of the world has a particular form.

A model represents some part of that structure for a particular problem. It may simplify, idealize, ignore variables, or hold others fixed.

An experiment creates a test situation.

An observation is what is encountered within that situation.

Compress it.

Their relation can be represented as:

E→M→X→O,E \rightarrow M \rightarrow X \rightarrow O,E→M→X→O,

where EEE is the explanation, MMM the model, XXX the experiment or test, and OOO the observation.

Criticism travels back through this chain cautiously:

criticism of O→criticism of X→criticism of M→ criticism of E⏟possibilities\underbrace { \text{criticism of O} \rightarrow \text{criticism of X} \rightarrow \text{criticism of M} \rightarrow \text{ criticism of E}}_{\text{possibilities}}possibilitiescriticism of O→criticism of X→criticism of M→ criticism of E​​

The word possibilities is load-bearing. An unexpected observation may reveal a bad measurement, a bad model, a false auxiliary assumption, a regime where an approximation fails, or a genuine explanatory failure. The observation does not announce which part of the structure broke. Everything is conjectural.

Two rival explanations can also produce nearly identical models over a restricted domain. Both may fit the same observations. If so, those observations do not discriminate between the explanations.

So:

model success≠explanatory truth.\text{model success} \neq \text{explanatory truth}.model success=explanatory truth.

This isn't just about science. The distinction becomes clearer when the example of everyday examples enters the argument.

3. Why Examples Feel Stronger Than They Are

Suppose I am trying to explain a novel abstraction to you:

epistemology changes how we explain people.

The sentence is too compressed. You may not directly possess the explanatory structure I have in mind. So you say:

“For example?”

Now I give you the personality-trait case, the lonely young man, the child in school. Each example removes some possible reconstructions of what I meant. The examples help you rebuild enough of the structure inside your own mind for the abstraction to become intelligible and criticizable.

That operation is useful enough to name. When one mind uses language, cases, and distinctions to let another mind conjecture an explanatory structure, the second mind is performing reconstruction.

The order matters. The examples do not mechanically generate the explanation. They constrain your attempt to reconstruct it.

Abstract it.

The logical relation:

O1, O2, O3O_1,\text{ }O_2,\text{ }O_3O1​, O2​, O3​

does not entail:

E.E.E.

The examples help you reconstruct EEE. They do not justify or confirm EEE.

So examples are epistemically irrelevant?

No. That would erase the very function we just identified. Examples can make an explanation communicable. They can reveal what a compressed abstraction means in practice. They can also participate in tests once an explanatory relation has been specified. What they cannot do merely by fitting is derive the explanation that makes them intelligible.

The same distinction becomes visible when an example is missing. Someone offers a high-reach explanation. You ask for an instance. The speaker struggles.

The failure may matter communicatively. It may diminish the rhetorical impact. Perhaps the abstraction is too vague to reconstruct. Perhaps the speaker has not connected it to any usable model. Those are real criticisms. But inability to improvise an example under conversational pressure does not by itself eliminate the explanation. Example formulation and explanatory content are different capacities.

Now the psychological problem sharpens.

Once enough examples fit, you begin to feel:

yes, I understand.

Then, easily:

yes, that is right.

Those are different achievements.

The first can mark successful reconstruction. The second is an epistemic judgment about the explanation.

The transition is easy to miss because the same mechanism that lets you inhabit another person's abstraction also produces a feeling of fit. A meeting of the minds. The better the reconstruction works, the more coherent the surrounding observations can appear.

Keep the relation and drop the incidental detail:

successful reconstruction≠successful error elimination.\text{successful reconstruction} \neq \text{successful error elimination}.successful reconstruction=successful error elimination.

The feeling of understanding is not meaningless. It may show that explanatory structure has actually been transmitted. But transmission and truth are different relations.

Observations make the transition easier because their descriptions are comparatively plastic. The same event can be described as:

The raven is black.

or:

The raven is not non-black.

The event has not changed. The linguistic surface has.

A more consequential case has the same structure. One feature of an intervention can be emphasized, another omitted. The same event can be embedded in rival narratives because the observation does not contain its own explanatory role.

Explanations are rigid in a different sense. They assert dependencies. If an explanation says:

A→B,A \rightarrow B,A→B,

then neither

B→AB \rightarrow AB→A

nor

¬A→¬B\neg A \rightarrow \neg B¬A→¬B

follows.

Explain.

The direction of dependence is part of the explanation. Rival explanations may accommodate the same observations while making incompatible claims about what depends on what. Their conflict appears in the structure that the observations alone leave underdetermined.

This suggests a neat procedure: assemble rival explanations, derive their consequences, and test where they diverge.

No. That begins too late.

Often there is enormous data, observation, correlation, and measurement while there is still no good explanation. The difficult part of knowledge creation is frequently creating even one conjecture with enough structure to criticize. It may be exceedingly difficult to formulate even one good explanation for a given problem situation.

Two serious explanatory rivals are often an epistemic luxury. Their incompatible dependencies may make sharp tests possible, but criticism does not require a menu.

If you have only one serious conjecture, criticism can still reach into its structure. A dependency may contradict another part of the explanation. An assumption may be doing work the explanation has not acknowledged. A predicted observation may fail to occur. The explanation may solve one problem only by losing reach somewhere else. The general requirement is not multiple options. It is vulnerability to error.

This is already enough to explain one part of the felt asymmetry. Confirmation can arrive through recognition: another fitting case, another successful reconstruction, another observation that can be placed inside the model. Criticism requires exposing the structure strongly enough for something in it to fail or become problematic.

4. Why Confirmation Is Cheap

The distinction between explanation and model offers a more precise account of memetic compression.

A complex explanation may contain many dependencies, qualifications, boundary conditions, unresolved criticisms, and rival interpretations. Carrying all of that explicitly is cognitively expensive. So objective knowledge is often compressed into something smaller: a phrase, a slogan, a category, a story, a memorable case.

Take:

people need strong leaders.

The sentence can stand in for a much larger explanatory structure about coordination, uncertainty, hierarchy, authority, competence, and social order. The compressed form is easier to remember, easier to transmit, and easier to instantiate. But it contains much less structure.

That tradeoff is unavoidable. Compression is not itself an error. You cannot keep every dependency of every explanation available to attention at once. The vulnerability appears when the compressed object stops functioning as an index into a larger explanation and begins functioning as the explanation itself.

Once that happens, confirming observations are readily captured.

The meme:

kids today are spoiled

finds instances.

The meme:

experts know best

finds instances.

The meme:

authorities cannot be trusted

finds instances.

The examples differ, but the mechanism is the same. The available compression organizes salience. Events that fit it are easy to classify and remember. The selected observations then return to consciousness already shaped by the model that made them salient.

Abstract it:

M→salience→Oselected→felt confirmation.M \rightarrow \text{salience} \rightarrow O_{\text{selected}} \rightarrow \text{felt confirmation}.M→salience→Oselected​→felt confirmation.

No formal induction is required. You only need to keep recognizing instances of an already available compression.

And then you show someone else. The mechanism does not stop at the boundary of one mind.

So if the meme is false:

Bullshit travels faster than the truth.

You just recalled a meme that makes a meme out of the Bullshit Asymmetry Principle.

Brandolini's law is not an epistemic law. It is a set of weakly tied conjectures. It asserts that falsehoods are usually simple and truth complex. That people prefer fast certainty over ambiguity. That media reward falsehood over accuracy, and that we can become overwhelmed in a glut of false claims.

But it does describe an asymmetrical epistemic environment. It is the recursive application of confirmation operating within our justified world. A single disconfirming observation may stop bullshit in its tracks.

I see what you did there.

Much ordinary communication consists of trying to make another person see what you have come to see:

You won't believe what I saw.

Did you see that?

You have to hear this.

An observation, story, or compressed explanation changes your epistemic landscape. You then try to reproduce some of that change in another mind. We do not need a biological theory of the compulsion in order to notice the structure. People persistently try to render actions, beliefs, and events intelligible to one another.

Someone behaves unexpectedly. You ask why. A friend makes an odd decision. You reconstruct the problem that might make the action intelligible. You do the same to yourself:

Why did I do that?

Why does this matter to me?

What am I actually worried about?

This activity is older and broader than the philosophical concept of justification. It is explanation-seeking as social reconstruction.

But explanation can slide into justification because intelligibility often feels like validation. When an action becomes understandable, the subjective change can be mistaken for a change in its epistemic or moral status. When a belief becomes reconstructable, the same thing can happen.

Now add agreement.

Suppose I tell you what I saw and you reply:

Exactly.

Two solitary minds have reconstructed compatible versions of the same abstraction. That is genuine social consilience: successful transmission between solitary minds.

But transmission is still not truth. Agreement may indicate shared background knowledge, shared compression, successful reconstruction, or shared error. Yet there is a large subjective difference between nobody seeing what you mean and everyone seeing it.

That difference gives justification a social charge. A successful compression can also become social currency. If a phrase, example, or story is memorable and easy to instantiate, recognition by others can bring status. The sender may appear perceptive, funny, morally alert, correct, or simply inside the group.

Truth-seeking and status-seeking do not need to be consciously opposed for this to matter. You can genuinely want others to see what you saw while also receiving status from successful transmission.

Social media changes the scale and speed of the mechanism. Recognition becomes visible, immediate, public, and countable through likes, shares, replies, followers, and reposts. Those signals are not epistemic validation. But they can become adjacent to the same subjective machinery.

Abstract it:

compression→propagation→recognition→status→more propagation.\text{compression} \rightarrow \text{propagation} \rightarrow \text{recognition} \rightarrow \text{status} \rightarrow \text{more propagation}.compression→propagation→recognition→status→more propagation.

A vivid example can therefore travel faster than the explanation that would determine what the example actually bears on. Public discourse becomes saturated with single instances: one video, one scandal, one image, one anecdote, one outrageous quotation. Each can be used as a lightweight model for a much larger class, and the relationship between observation and explanation is not 1:1.

The informational environment changes the costs. Compression is cheap. Propagation is cheap. Recognition is rewarded. Reconstructing a large explanatory surface and exposing it to criticism remain comparatively expensive. The result can be an environment rich in apparent confirmation and poor in explanatory criticism.

No social mind is required. The epistemic act still occurs inside solitary minds. The network changes frequency, salience, cost, and reward. Each individual still reconstructs the compression for themselves.

This gives us a biological, cultural, and technological route by which confirmation can become easy to feel.

It still leaves one question untouched:

Why does criticism have any special epistemic force at all?

5. The Asymmetry

The answer is logical rather than psychological.

Suppose an explanation entails an observable consequence:

E→O.E \rightarrow O.E→O.

If the test yields:

¬O,\neg O,¬O,

then, assuming the relevant premises of the test are retained:

¬E\neg E¬E

follows by modus tollens.

What?

The inference is deductive. If the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false.

Reverse the result.

Suppose the explanation entails OOO, and you observe OOO:

E→OE \rightarrow OE→O

and

O.O.O.

Those premises do not entail EEE. Other explanations may also entail OOO. Inferring EEE would affirm the consequent.

That is the asymmetry. It is not a preference for negative thinking. It is not a personality trait. It is not a cultural taste for criticism.

But experiments often succeed. That matters. The error is in saying what the success does.

If an explanation survives a severe attempted falsification, we may call it corroborated. The term records that the explanation survived criticism that could have exposed a problem. It does not mean the matching observation derived the explanation, added truth to it, or justified it.

Corroboration is survival of criticism, not accumulated support. This leaves empirical rigor intact. Rejecting justification does not require abandoning experiment, measurement, observation, prediction, statistics, model comparison, or replication. Those practices can expose discrepancies, characterize restricted problems, and eliminate false models.

What changes is their epistemic interpretation. A measurement can reveal a failure. A statistical method can describe a represented problem. An observation can participate in a test. None supplies a deductive route from finite instances to universal explanatory truth. The tools remain. The justificatory interpretation is what disappears.

There is also a positive constraint that does not reduce to falsification.

An explanation about one part of reality exists in the same world as explanations about everything else. If it solves one problem only by contradicting powerful explanations elsewhere, the conflict itself becomes a problem. Compatibility across explanatory domains will be called consilience.

Consilience and falsification therefore do different work. Falsification asks where an explanatory structure breaks under a test. Consilience asks whether the structure can coexist with other powerful explanations of the same reality without hidden contradiction.

Neither justifies the explanation. Both constrain it.

The distinction matters reflexively because epistemology has universal reach. The same explanation has been carried through behavioral genetics, psychology, society, institutions, freedom, abstraction, consilience, criticism, problem creation, coordination, evil, and now justification.

That increasing coherence can feel compelling. It may be genuine consilience. It may also generate exactly the misleading feeling this essay is trying to criticize.

So the relevant test is not:

How many more examples fit?

It is:

Where does the explanation break?

That question returns us to probability, because a tool can become hardest to criticize precisely when it disappears into ordinary thought.

6. When a Tool Disappears into Thought

Formal probability is objective knowledge. Once integrated into cognition, however, it can stop appearing as an external technique and begin structuring the questions that seem natural.

You may find yourself asking automatically:

  • how likely is this?
  • what is the confidence level?
  • what probability should I assign?
  • which option has the highest expected value?

Within represented spaces, those questions can be exactly the right ones.

The danger appears when the question itself is open. A probabilistic frame can then import assumptions that have not earned their place. The relevant options are treated as known, the variables as stable, the evaluative criterion as fixed, and the hypothesis space as already containing what matters.

The tool becomes invisible. The problem is then reformulated until it fits the tool. Modern data environments intensify that pressure. They produce measurements, metrics, behavioral traces, sensor data, correlations, and forecasts in volumes that earlier cultures could not produce.

Observation can therefore become abundant while explanation remains scarce. A field can measure more and more without having a better answer to:

What is actually going on?

That distinction creates a technological waypoint.

Inductive and probabilistic methods may offer predictions. But if explanatory knowledge is not logically derivable from prior observations, then predictive success alone does not explain the universal explanatory capability attributed to persons.

If AGI is to possess that epistemic capability, the problem is not merely:

how does the system predict well?

A further problem appears:

how does the system create explanatory knowledge that is not logically derivable from the observations already represented?

Nothing here settles competing AI programs. The distinction is narrower. Inductive competence and explanatory creativity are not the same explanatory object.

Now the technological problem folds back into the cultural one. Current AI systems were created inside societies saturated with support, evidence, confidence, argument, defense, consensus, probability, and optimization. Their training material contains essays defending theses, legal arguments, political rhetoric, marketing, academic papers, textbooks, apologetics, social media, and opinion.

Much recorded language is justificatory in form. So AI inherits more than objective knowledge. It also inherits cultural forms for defending what is already being said.

Turn that into a mechanism.

A user supplies a compression: a belief, thesis, suspicion, ideology, diagnosis, or preferred explanation. The system can search the available objective knowledge for material that can be attached to it: examples, arguments, analogies, citations, historical parallels, theoretical vocabulary, reconciliations of apparent contradictions. The output becomes richer. The original compression remains unchanged. The user experiences increasing confirmation.

AI becomes a justification machine. AI automates justificationism.

7. AI in Both Directions

The asymmetry is economic as well as logical. You can ask a machine for twenty supporting arguments, twenty examples, ten sources, several analogies, a stronger rhetorical version, and a more sophisticated theoretical defense in very little time. The cost of generating reasons collapses.

But genuine criticism still requires something more specific than producing the opposite list. The explanatory structure has to be exposed. A dependency has to fail. An assumption has to be located. A rival explanation has to make a different dependency visible. A model boundary has to matter to the problem.

A system that makes support abundant can therefore amplify confirmation without changing the logic of confirmation at all. That is one direction.

Now reverse it.

AI is treated as a technology of retrieval, synthesis, compression, and reorganization. The present problem reveals another possible function.

A large explanation exceeds what you can keep available in working attention. It may contain many dependencies, qualifications, rival accounts, historical revisions, unresolved criticisms, and model boundaries. Unaided, you compress.

Until recently the practical choice was often between carrying a small compression biologically and relying on an external authority, text, or institution to carry more of the structure.

AI creates another possibility: externalize a larger explanatory surface and recover the relevant parts when the problem calls for them.

This is more than memory of propositions. What matters is preserving relations. Which claims depend on which others? Which assumptions are local? Where does a model stop working? Which criticism remains unresolved? Which rival explanation covers the same observation differently?

An external system that preserves those relations can function as explanatory memory. That term names a specific capability, a specific epistemic medium. The machine stores or reconstructs enough of the explanatory surface that a local compression can later be reopened for criticism.

Compression therefore remains necessary but need not be irreversible:

E→McE \rightarrow M_cE→Mc​

for local cognitive use, while a route back remains available:

Mc↔E.M_c \leftrightarrow E.Mc​↔E.

The bi-directionality changes what compression can do. A meme can lead back to the larger explanation. An example can lead back to the dependency it instantiates. A slogan can be expanded into claims, assumptions, boundaries, and rival accounts.

Compress and decompress.

That operation matters especially for large inherited abstract structures: religions, political ideologies, moral systems, economic theories, legal traditions. Few individuals can reconstruct such systems at full resolution. Historically, authorities, canonical texts, doctrines, summaries, slogans, and interpreters carried parts of the explanatory burden. That solved a real cognitive-cost problem. Someone or something outside you carried structure you could not keep active yourself.

It also created a route to closure: the verdict could arrive before the structure that made the verdict criticizable. AI can lower the cost of asking for that structure back.

You can ask which claims depend on which others, which parts are empirical, which are moral, which are historical, where rival explanations cover the same observations, and where those explanations actually diverge.

This is decompression: not recovery of some perfect original, but expansion of a compressed representation into a richer explanatory structure with more explicit points of possible criticism.

The relation to authority changes because you can increasingly request:

Show me the explanation, not merely the verdict.

This does not make expertise irrelevant. It changes how much explanatory surface can be made available to the singular person confronting a problem. To you.

Social media and AI therefore create opposite transformations around the same object. Social media made compression and propagation extraordinarily cheap:

explanation→meme→mass propagation.\text{explanation} \rightarrow \text{meme} \rightarrow \text{mass propagation}.explanation→meme→mass propagation.

AI can sometimes make the reverse traversal cheaper:

meme→expanded model→underlying explanations→criticism.\text{meme} \rightarrow \text{expanded model} \rightarrow \text{underlying explanations} \rightarrow \text{criticism}.meme→expanded model→underlying explanations→criticism.

The symmetry is imperfect. AI can also generate memes, propaganda, and confirmation loops. The capability does not determine its use.

But a new operation is available. You can take a viral example and ask what explanation would make the observation relevant. Then the structure can be reopened: what rival explanation accommodates it equally well, which assumptions have been compressed away, and does the example actually discriminate between explanations?

AI can also reduce the cost of holding rivals open. You often settle on one working explanation because maintaining several large alternatives is expensive. An external system can preserve explanation A, explanation B, explanation C, together with their assumptions, incompatibilities, domains of success, and shared observations.

The resulting question changes from:

Which one currently feels most coherent?

to:

Where do they diverge?

That can extend your epistemic reach. It does not settle the status of the machine.

AI can carry objective knowledge, reorganize explanatory structure, generate candidate criticisms, preserve alternatives, and reopen compressed models. None of those capabilities alone establishes that the machine is an AGI.

The epistemic subject in this inquiry remains you: the individual for whom something is a problem, who can reject the framing, evaluate criticism, and create the next problem. The machine can enlarge the explanatory surface available to you. It does not thereby become the solitary knower whose problem the surface serves.

To think otherwise is to misunderstand what our confirming observations can support, without an explanation for how.

8. You Are Inside the Asymmetry

The same technology can therefore participate in opposite epistemic processes.

As justification:

AI→more support→closure.\text{AI} \rightarrow \text{more support} \rightarrow \text{closure}.AI→more support→closure.

As explanatory memory:

AI→more preserved structure→more opportunities for criticism.\text{AI} \rightarrow \text{more preserved structure} \rightarrow \text{more opportunities for criticism}.AI→more preserved structure→more opportunities for criticism.

The technology does not determine which process occurs. The difference appears in the problem you bring to it and in what happens to the explanatory structure afterward.

If the initial belief remains fixed, the system can be asked to find support, reconcile objections, and generate confirming cases. The explanatory surface expands while the underlying compression remains protected.

The operation can be reversed. The system can expose dependencies, recover model boundaries, preserve rivals, and identify where an explanation could fail. The same increase in available structure now creates more points of criticism.

The distinction is not between optimistic and pessimistic uses of AI. It is between operations that increase closure and operations that increase vulnerability to error.

Return to you. Why does justification feel natural? Several mechanisms now converge.

Objective knowledge carries justificatory traditions. Formal probability supplies powerful reasoning inside represented spaces and can become so cognitively familiar that its boundary conditions disappear from view. Examples help solitary minds reconstruct one another's abstractions. Successful reconstruction produces a real feeling of fit. Compression makes large explanatory structures cheap enough to remember and transmit. Once a compression is available, salience supplies further instances. Social recognition rewards successful transmission. Social media quantifies that reward. AI can make supporting reasons abundant almost without limit.

None of these mechanisms creates the missing logical entailment from matching observation to explanatory truth. Together they may explain why the absence of that entailment need not be psychologically obvious.

This also explains why criticism can require effort without treating that effort as evidence of a defect in human nature.

The machinery doing ordinary cognitive work solves other problems. Reconstruction lets you understand another mind. Compression reduces cognitive cost. Stable models make action possible. Shared abstractions permit coordination. Recognition helps information propagate. Probabilistic tools solve real closed-space problems.

Criticism can work against the local success of those mechanisms. It can require you to reopen a compression that is already useful, expose an assumption that stabilizes action, or preserve a rival that makes coherence harder to maintain. It can also disturb a social identity built around the compression, destabilize social membership organized around it, reopen a problem that had seemed settled, or destroy a simplification that was doing useful work.

The cost is therefore intelligible. Criticism is a cultural technology for forcing explanatory structures back into contact with possible error.

Now turn the criticism back on this essay.

By this point, the epistemology itself may feel increasingly natural. The examples fit. The topics cohere. Probability, models, memes, institutions, and AI now appear inside one explanatory structure.

That may indicate reach. It may indicate internal consilience. It may indicate that the abstractions have been successfully reconstructed. It may also produce the very feeling of confirmation the chapter has been explaining.

The distinction cannot be resolved by adding more fitting examples.

The live question remains:

Where does it break?

Anti-justificationism can sound like subtraction: no certainty, no induction, no probabilistic foundation, no confirmation. But the resulting epistemology is not empty. Far from it.

Problems still constrain inquiry because some attempted solutions fail to solve them. Explanations still assert dependencies that can contradict observations, other explanations, or themselves. Models still have boundaries. Experiments still create situations in which failures can become visible. Measurements still reveal discrepancies. Statistical methods still characterize represented structures. Corroboration still records survival of severe criticism. Consilience still exposes conflicts between explanations that claim to describe one reality.

The rigor remains because error remains possible and because explanatory structure can be exposed to it. What disappears is the claim that rigor comes from accumulating support until belief becomes entitled.

AI changes the practical balance around that rigor. For most of history, large explanatory structures were expensive for an individual to carry. Authorities, institutions, texts, doctrines, and compressed cultural forms preserved more than one person could keep available at once.

Now a singular person, you, can increasingly interrogate explanatory surfaces that would previously have remained inaccessible. You can ask for the structure behind a slogan or the rival account behind a disagreement. You can reopen the model boundary behind an observation, the assumption behind a probability, or the unresolved criticism behind a confident summary.

That does not guarantee openness. The same system can produce a denser confirmation loop. But the available operation has changed.

The epistemic promise of AI may therefore be less about giving you better answers than about making it cheaper to keep more epistemic structure open to criticism at once.

This essay itself emerged through that kind of movement. It did not begin with a finished architecture.

It began with a problem:

anti-justificationism conflicts with the felt naturalness of confirmation.

The first attempt could have stopped with a familiar answer: people seek certainty. That did not explain enough.

This essay was not justified into existence. It was an attempt to solve a problem, and the attempt remains provisional. Its reach may grow. Its consilience may grow. Neither protects it from your criticism. The next criticism is what could create the next problem.

What is the next problem?

If criticism is a learned orientation, and if AI can make both justification and criticism dramatically cheaper, then the successor problem is no longer only how knowledge grows.

It is:

How can you engage with objective knowledge while remaining open to criticism that might change the very problem you're trying to solve?
