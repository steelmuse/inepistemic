What Moves You

XVIII. Unpredictable You

Creativity appears throughout the epistemic cycle: in problems, tentative solutions, criticism, successor problems, and the transitions among them.

Fallibility travels with it. A creative problem can be a bad problem. A creative criticism can be mistaken. A transformed evaluative criterion can make an explanation worse. A novel transition can move you into a more epistemically closed world.

Creativity is not a guarantee of progress. It is the capacity to bring new explanatory structure into the world.

That capacity suggests a problem: If genuinely new explanatory knowledge can appear, then the future epistemic landscape does not yet exist. We can know an enormous amount about the present. But the knowledge that will change the world is not yet available.

The problem is therefore deeper than uncertainty. The future is not merely hidden; it is open. And if people create the knowledge from which later history will be made, then we are not merely living inside an unpredictable world. We are among the causes of its inherent unpredictability.

1. Creativity Is Distributed Through the Cycle

The schematic can tempt us into assigning creativity to one place:

P1→TS→EE→P2.P_1 \rightarrow \mathbf{TS} \rightarrow EE \rightarrow P_2.P1​→TS→EE→P2​.

The problem is given, you invent a solution, the solution is criticized, and the cycle continues. That picture is too simple.

You can live for years inside an inherited problem and then realize that the problem was badly formed. The question changes. The evaluative criteria change. What once counted as progress becomes irrelevant. You have not merely produced another answer; you have created a new region in which answers can exist.

Criticism can do the same. It need not merely eliminate a tentative solution. It can reveal that the original opposition was false or expose an assumption that neither side had noticed. It can also connect two previously distant explanations and create a contradiction that did not previously exist for either participant. The successor problem is then not simply the old problem with one bad answer removed. It is new knowledge.

The arrows matter too. You can move from one problem to another without a complete explicit account of why. A memory becomes relevant, a distinction becomes salient, an old irritation becomes a problem, or something that had seemed settled becomes unstable. We do not yet possess a complete causal explanation of these transitions. But we can already reject one simplifying picture: creativity is not a module that activates when you need an idea. Nor is it a special mode reserved for artists, scientists, inventors, or children. It is implicated in the entire growth of knowledge.

That also means creativity does not always lead to progress. You can invent a better explanation or a better rationalization. You can create a criticism that exposes error or one whose function is to prevent another criticism from being heard. You can create a moral category that enlarges sympathy or one that makes exclusion easier. You can invent a tool for freedom or a tool for surveillance. The epistemic capacity is the same. The direction differs.

2. The Future Knowledge Problem

Suppose someone asks:

What will people know fifty years from now?

The question sounds ordinary. We may list likely technologies, extrapolate scientific trends, forecast political arrangements, or describe future cities, medicine, education, artificial intelligence, energy systems, relationships, and institutions. We may give probabilities or construct scenarios.

But the deepest problem appears before any of those exercises begin. If the future knowledge is genuinely new, then we do not yet possess the explanation that would make it available for prediction. To specify the explanatory content of the future discovery now would be, in the relevant sense, to have already created it.

This is not merely a shortage of data, insufficient computing power, or the complexity of the future. The future explanation does not yet exist as objective knowledge. That is why the growth of knowledge is intrinsically unpredictable.

A new explanation has a causal history. You can struggle with a problem for months while your skills change, your attention shifts, a criticism weakens an old explanation, and a new abstraction slowly becomes available. Several apparently unrelated regions of your epistemic mind can change incrementally. Then you say:

I just realized something.

The explicit appearance can be discontinuous even when the causal preparation is not.

The same structure can appear outside your solitary mind. A scientific revolution may later be narrated as a leap. A technological platform may seem to arrive suddenly. A moral transformation may be attached to one speech, one book, one court decision, one protest, or one invention.

Yet the visible event can emerge from countless smaller changes distributed among many loosely coordinated persons. Someone invents a tool. Someone else develops a notation. Someone elsewhere creates a criticism. An institution changes, a technical constraint disappears, a concept becomes culturally legible, or a problem that once seemed local becomes general. Eventually one visible artifact reorganizes the landscape.

The historical surface looks discontinuous because the causal history is distributed. Openness therefore does not require magic. It requires only that genuinely new explanatory structures can be created through causally continuous processes.

3. How Prediction Closes the World

The impossibility of predicting future knowledge is easy to state and difficult to inhabit because common sense pulls in the other direction. We look backward, see patterns, extend them, and call this experience. History seems to teach us how history behaves.

The familiar phrase is:

History does not repeat, but it rhymes.

The phrase denies crude repetition, but the deeper epistemic structure remains:

past pattern→future pattern.\text{past pattern} \rightarrow \text{future pattern}.past pattern→future pattern.

The rhyme is still supposed to be available in the past. The future remains a transformed continuation of an already represented possibility space.

Modern predictive reasoning can look as though it escapes that structure. Instead of saying, "This happened before, therefore it will happen again," we now say something more precise: "This happened before, so the posterior probability should increase." The formal tools are more powerful too. The epistemic move can nevertheless remain the same: represent a possibility space, assign probabilities within it, and use new observations to update those probabilities.

This can be extremely useful inside a closed enough world. The problem begins when the representation is promoted into an account of knowledge creation itself. A probability distribution operates over represented possibilities, while knowledge creation can alter what the possibilities are. You can create a new option, reject the original categories, or redefine the problem. You can invent a criterion that makes the previous ranking meaningless or produce an explanation showing that the original possibility space was badly formed.

The progression becomes more sophisticated: from "history repeats" to "history rhymes," then to probabilistic resemblance and updating credence as confirming observations arrive. The problem remains.

The same inversion appears when a useful model becomes a theory of the mind using it. The Bayesian model becomes the Bayesian brain. A person who can employ a probabilistic representation is redescribed as though their epistemic life consists of assigning and updating weights over an already specified world. The categorical error: a psychometric compression becomes personality, a diagnostic compression becomes the person, a statistical regularity becomes a causal ontology. Now a probabilistic model of represented uncertainty becomes a model of knowledge creation itself.

The tool is real, and so is its usefulness. The promotion is the error.

Once a projected future exists, observations can begin to accumulate around it. A predicted trend continues, so confidence increases. An event resembles the scenario, so the model appears corroborated. A contrary observation arrives and is classified as noise, temporary deviation, or an exception anticipated by another branch of the model. The representational structure grows more elaborate, and its apparent credibility increases because it can absorb more of what happens.

Abstract it.

The sequence can become:

extrapolation→future model→credence→confirming observations→greater credence.\text{extrapolation} \rightarrow \text{future model} \rightarrow \text{credence} \rightarrow \text{confirming observations} \rightarrow \text{greater credence}.extrapolation→future model→credence→confirming observations→greater credence.

This is deeper than confirmation bias as a psychological tendency. The future model has been granted the epistemic status of a space within which later observations are interpreted. The closed world becomes the frame. You then move through imagined histories, updating priors and counting confirmations inside a representation whose most important omission is the knowledge that does not yet exist.

Such a model may be mathematically sophisticated. It may generate precise outputs, survive many observations, and support useful decisions. None of that changes the status of the represented possibility space itself: it remains conjectural. A new technology can invalidate the category by which a trend was represented. A moral criticism can transform what later people regard as an acceptable outcome. A new institution can change the relevant action space. A scientific explanation can show that the variables in the original model were badly chosen.

The failure, then, need not be that the forecast was slightly wrong. The represented future can cease to be the relevant future.

4. You Are Not a Projection of Your Present Compression

The argument becomes more intimate when we return to you and your solitary mind. That mind includes explicit explanations, but also inexplicit knowledge, memory, skill, salience, affect, expectation, perceptual organization, concepts, language, social knowledge, unfinished problems, and changing evaluative criteria. No outside observer possesses direct access to this vast universe, and you do not possess explicit access to it either.

Yet we regularly compress the epistemic mind, MiM_iMi​. We call someone conscientious, anxious, agreeable, gifted, difficult, traditional, impulsive, depressed, creative, uncreative, a leader, a follower, a risk, or a type. Some of these compressions can be useful. The epistemic error appears when compression becomes projection:

Mi,t0→M^i,t0→M^i,t1→M^i,t2.M_{i,t_0} \rightarrow \hat{M}_{i,t_0} \rightarrow \hat{M}_{i,t_1} \rightarrow \hat{M}_{i,t_2}.Mi,t0​​→M^i,t0​​→M^i,t1​​→M^i,t2​​.

You are first made simple in state and then simple in evolution. Your future is predicted from the compression.

The problem is not only that categories fail to capture everything about you. The dimensions discarded by the category may include the very knowledge creation through which you become different. You become predictable in the model because the model first makes you smaller—not smaller in reality, but smaller in the representation.

The moral consequence becomes clearer when the compression concerns another person. Imagine that I construct an explanation of them: selfish, unreliable, ideological, anxious, narcissistic, timid, ambitious. Perhaps the description captures something observable. But once the category becomes stable, it begins to travel forward. A generous act becomes strategic. A disagreement becomes diagnostic. A change of mind becomes inconsistency. A surprising action becomes evidence for a deeper version of the same trait.

The model begins to absorb its own criticism. The other person becomes increasingly predictable because the explanation has become increasingly difficult to escape. The problem is not merely that I may be wrong. I have reduced the other person's epistemic openness before encountering it.

A broader approach begins by keeping the explanation exposed to failure. Suppose the person acts in a way I do not understand. Instead of asking:

What kind of person behaves like this?

I can ask:

What problem would make this action intelligible?

That question produces several tentative reconstructions:

A→{P1,P2,P3,…,Pn}.A \rightarrow \{P_1,P_2,P_3,\ldots,P_n\}.A→{P1​,P2​,P3​,…,Pn​}.

Perhaps the person is solving a status problem, a safety problem, or a loyalty problem. Perhaps they are responding to a moral problem I do not yet recognize. Perhaps my description of the action is itself wrong. One of these models need not secretly be the true one. Their value here is that the wider representational surface creates more opportunities for my explanation to fail.

This is the rationality principle used as a surrogate rather than a prophecy. The model says:

If this were the person's problem situation, the action would make sense.

It does not say:

Therefore this is what the person will do next.

Its epistemic value comes from becoming criticizable. The observed person remains able to destroy the reconstruction. To explore another person's mind in this way is therefore also a moral practice. It refuses premature closure and treats the other person as a universe that cannot be exhausted by the compression through which they first became legible to us.

5. How Openness Gets Recruited Against Itself

Children make epistemic openness difficult to ignore. A child can turn a chair into a spacecraft. A question about an insect can become a question about death. A game can change its criteria halfway through. A child can ask a question that exposes an assumption adults stopped noticing years ago.

The claim is not that childhood is a mystical state of pure creativity. Children are fallible. Their problems can be confused, their explanations wrong, their criticisms arbitrary, and their inventions unable to survive contact with reality. What childhood makes conspicuous is open-ended epistemic movement.

Coordination changes the environment in which that movement occurs. Schools need schedules, curricula, assessments, standards, classrooms, roles, and expected outputs. Organizations need employees to perform identifiable tasks. Professions need credentials. Bureaucracies need forms. Coordination therefore requires compression.

A child who freely creates problems enters environments in which the problem is often supplied in advance. The solution space narrows, the evaluation criteria become externally legible, and the transition schedule is imposed.

Abstract it.

The sequence can become:

assigned problem→permitted solution→authorized criticism→next assigned problem.\text{assigned problem} \rightarrow \text{permitted solution} \rightarrow \text{authorized criticism} \rightarrow \text{next assigned problem}.assigned problem→permitted solution→authorized criticism→next assigned problem.

This does not eliminate creativity. If it did, communication itself would become impossible. You still have to interpret instructions, reconstruct meanings, resolve ambiguities, form expectations, and integrate new information into an epistemic mind no institution possesses. The creative process continues, but its direction can be constrained and recruited.

Creativity can even be used to impede further creativity. A manager can invent a more sophisticated performance system that makes dissent harder. A bureaucracy can create increasingly elaborate categories through which people must become legible. A political movement can create a vocabulary that turns criticism into evidence of disloyalty. An institution can create procedures whose complexity makes responsibility difficult to locate. Objective knowledge can preserve and scale these creative attempts to suppress further creativity.

The same structure can operate inwardly. You encounter an inherited explanation of what a successful person should be. You do not merely submit mechanically; you can creatively reconstruct yourself within it. You explain why your interests are impractical and reinterpret dissatisfaction as immaturity. You explain away a problem that threatens an identity and invent reasons that the life expected of you is the life you really wanted. The resulting self-explanation may become extraordinarily elaborate.

Creativity has not disappeared. It has been recruited into epistemic closure.

That is why saying "I am not creative" can distort what is happening. If personhood involves creating problems, explanations, criticisms, interpretations, and transitions among epistemic states, then artistic creativity is one conspicuous expression of a more general epistemic capacity. The issue is not that everyone is secretly an artist. It is that your epistemic life already depends upon forms of creation that a narrow cultural category of creativity may fail to name.

6. The Geometry of Openness

The distinction between compression and openness now recurs at several scales. The error has the same shape each time: compress an open present, project the compression, and mistake continuity in the model for knowledge of what will become.

That recurring geometry sharpens the question we can ask of objective knowledge. At t0t_0t0​, our representational surface is extraordinarily broad. We possess objective knowledge about physical systems, biological processes, technologies, institutions, laws, markets, languages, software, mathematics, scientific explanations, engineering systems, historical records, norms, organizational processes, and innumerable local causal structures.

The breadth is vast, but the depth of legitimate causal projection into an epistemically open future is comparatively shallow. We can represent many things near the present. We cannot represent the future knowledge that will later reorganize them. The epistemic landscape may therefore look less like a narrow road extending far into the future. It may look more like an enormous surface lying close to the present: very broad, relatively shallow.

Much of our representational activity points in the opposite direction. We build narrow explanatory surfaces and project them many steps forward. Economic forecasts, political forecasts, technology forecasts, demographic forecasts, market forecasts, risk scenarios, strategic roadmaps, and histories of the future all take this form. The representation has depth; the causal basis may not. The farther the projection travels through domains saturated by human knowledge creation, the more opportunities there are for explanatory novelty to alter the path. The future representation grows thick while the epistemic foundation grows thin.

Consilience points across the broad surface instead. If there is one reality, explanations developed in different domains cannot remain permanently incompatible. A biological explanation can collide with a psychological explanation. A computational explanation can collide with a theory of mind. A moral explanation can collide with an institutional practice. A statistical model can collide with an explanation of the person. The collision creates a problem.

This is one reason creating a genuinely good explanation is difficult. An explanation may look excellent inside the narrow surface where it was invented; its weakness becomes visible only when its consequences reach another domain. The breadth of objective knowledge therefore creates an enormous error-elimination surface. More knowledge does not merely mean more facts. It means more opportunities for explanations to collide.

The direction of epistemic progress is therefore not necessarily:

deeper projection.\text{deeper projection}.deeper projection.

It may be:

greater breadth→greater consilience→more contradiction→better problems.\text{greater breadth} \rightarrow \text{greater consilience} \rightarrow \text{more contradiction} \rightarrow \text{better problems}.greater breadth→greater consilience→more contradiction→better problems.

This is not a retreat from explanation. It is a different use of explanatory reach. Present categories receive less authority over the distant future, while present explanations face more pressure to survive contact with everything else we already know.

7. AI Depth or Breadth

Modern computation gives us extraordinary representational scale. A machine can retrieve, transform, compare, and preserve more objective knowledge than one biological mind can hold explicitly, and artificial intelligence extends that scale dramatically.

One obvious use of that capacity is to project represented structure forward. We ask machines to forecast, rank, score, predict, estimate, assign probability, generate scenarios, project trends, and model future behavior. The machine receives a possibility space, extends it, assigns weights, elaborates consequences, and generates a future that appears increasingly detailed. This can make epistemic thinness look like knowledge.

A model begins with a few assumptions. The machine produces ten pages of consequences. The output is coherent, specific, internally connected, perhaps quantitative. The original assumptions disappear beneath the representational richness. A thin future looks thick.

Generative AI is unusually well matched to this operation because continuation is one of its conspicuous abilities. Give it a sequence and it can continue the sequence. Give it a trend and it can extend the trend. Give it a set of political assumptions and it can generate future political scenarios. Give it a technological roadmap and it can populate the decades around it. Give it a profile of a person and it can produce imagined future behavior.

The output can be compelling because it resembles the structure of historical explanation. Events lead to events. Causes accumulate. Institutions respond. People adapt. Technology advances. The future becomes narratively legible. But narrative legibility is not future knowledge.

The machine has extended inherited structure. It has not thereby created the explanatory knowledge that future persons will later create. This is the external analogue of predicting the compressed person: a narrow representation is projected forward, the generative system fills the empty surface, and openness disappears inside prose.

Probabilistic machinery can make the projection look firmer still. Scenario A receives 50%, Scenario B 30%, Scenario C 20%. The imagined future now has numerical credence as well as narrative detail. New events can be interpreted as movement toward one scenario or another, and the future is progressively redescribed as though it had always been contained in the original model. The history of the future is closed.

But computation need not be used primarily for depth. A model can make an explanation precise, expose a dependency, show what would follow if a conjecture were correct, and make criticism easier. The rationality principle is useful for exactly this reason. A model of another person can say:

If this were their problem situation, this action would make sense.

Reality can contradict that reconstruction. The same logic can operate at larger scales. A model of an institution can say:

If these constraints are operative, this outcome should follow.

A model of a technology can say:

If this causal architecture is correct, this intervention should produce that effect.

The model is useful because it creates a surface for error elimination. The mistake is to promote that surface into prophecy.

This changes the computational problem. Instead of asking which represented future is most probable, the machine can help expose which presently available explanations cannot all be true together. Instead of predicting what another person will do, it can generate several problem situations that would make an action intelligible. It can then identify what would disconfirm each reconstruction. Instead of asking which technology will dominate in 2040, it can expose the present constraints, explanations, and unresolved contradictions that make current trajectories unstable. Instead of narrating the future of education, it can place explanations of learning, authority, childhood, measurement, and knowledge creation beside one another and reveal where they conflict.

This does not make the future more predictable. It makes the present more criticizable.

Artificial intelligence may be particularly useful here because biological cognition is narrow at the explicit surface. You cannot simultaneously hold every relevant theory, paper, institutional rule, historical example, technical constraint, philosophical implication, and conflicting explanation. Objective knowledge already extends you beyond those limits: writing extends memory, mathematics extends representation, libraries extend retrieval, scientific institutions extend specialization, and computers extend calculation.

AI can extend comparison. It can place explanatory structures beside one another that no individual would otherwise hold together long enough for criticism to operate. It can help reveal hidden contradiction and generate alternative rational reconstructions. It can expose where a model depends on a category inherited from somewhere else, or where an explanation in one field quietly requires something another field says is impossible.

That is consilience as an engineering problem. You remain the epistemic locus; the machine enlarges the surface on which problems can appear.

The unpredictability of knowledge therefore suggests a research program. How can large bodies of objective knowledge be represented in ways that preserve explanatory structure rather than merely textual similarity? How can machines expose contradictions across fields? How can they generate criticizable rational reconstructions without converting those reconstructions into predictions? How can they preserve multiple candidate explanations without collapsing them into a probability distribution? How can they help you discover problems rather than merely answer inherited ones? How can they make conflicting evaluative criteria visible? How can they show where an apparently stable future depends on the assumption that nobody creates a new explanation?

These are technological questions, but they arise from the epistemology.

8. Planning Without Possessing the Future

The inability to know the future is not a new human problem. Traditions have located the future in fate, providence, prophecy, divine will, historical necessity, technological progress, economic law, and statistical expectation. The explanations differ enormously, but many can be reconstructed as responses to the same problem:

How should you act when what happens next cannot be known?

One recurring response is to create an authority with privileged access to the future. The role can be occupied by the oracle, the priest, the ruler, the historian of destiny, the expert forecaster, the probabilistic model, or the algorithm. The forms differ, but the attraction is similar. If the future can be known by someone or something, uncertainty becomes governable.

The epistemic problem returns wherever future history depends upon explanatory knowledge not yet created. No authority possesses that knowledge in advance. An authority may still know more about some present causal structures, construct better models, or identify real constraints. None of these achievements converts open knowledge creation into a closed future.

This does not make expertise useless or planning incoherent. You still act. Institutions coordinate. Engineers design. Companies allocate resources. Governments build infrastructure. Parents make decisions. The openness of knowledge changes the epistemic status of those activities rather than eliminating them.

A plan is a tentative solution. A roadmap is a conjecture. A scenario is a model. A forecast is a representation. Each can be useful and each can be criticized. None contains the future.

Planning under openness therefore has a different architecture. Action proceeds from the best present explanation while alternatives remain available, assumptions and causal dependencies remain visible, and the structures carrying the plan remain revisable. Surprise then becomes a new problem for the plan rather than a failure of reality to obey it. The aim is not possession of the future. It is continued capacity for correction when the future becomes different from what was represented.

The same structure applies to the future self.

You can create a plan for your life, form an identity, decide what matters, and imagine who you will become. These can all be useful forms of objective knowledge. But when you later inhabit that future, you will know things you do not know now. You may encounter problems that do not yet exist or acquire concepts that make current goals look strange. You may create a criticism of an identity that presently feels obvious. You may invent a criterion by which today's success looks like failure, or become interested in something you cannot yet name.

Your future self therefore cannot be fully represented by your present self. This is not merely because life is uncertain. It is because you remain epistemically open.

The same mistake we make with history can be made with ourselves: compress the present self, project the compression, call the trajectory identity. Commitments need not disappear for that mistake to be avoided. They can remain tentative structures inside an open epistemic life. Your future remains available for surprise because you remain capable of creating knowledge that your present representation cannot contain.

9. You Cause The Future

You are not merely inadequately described by a trait, more complicated than a category, or difficult to measure. You are a source of new explanatory knowledge.

Your future therefore cannot be represented merely as the continuation of your present state. And because people create the objective knowledge from which institutions, technologies, norms, sciences, and later problems are made, their creations inherit the same openness.

The future is not open because reality lacks causes. It is open because some of those causes will be knowledge that does not yet exist.

That gives creativity its most consequential meaning. You can bring into the world an explanation that changes what happens next. You can create a problem that redirects a field or a criticism that destroys an inherited category. You can create a criterion that makes an old success irrelevant, a tool that changes which actions are possible, or an explanation of yourself that reopens a life.

The openness of knowledge is therefore not merely an epistemic limitation. It is the causal signature of personhood.

You cannot know every problem your future self will encounter or every criterion by which you will judge what matters. Yet you must still act, commit, choose, and explain why one direction is worth pursuing rather than another.

Some of those explanations become so important that you stop treating them as explanations at all. You call them values.

But if your values are guides to an open future, what happens when they begin protecting themselves from the criticism that could change it?
