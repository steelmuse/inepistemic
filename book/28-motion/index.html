Where Knowledge Becomes

XXVIII. You Are a Deep Loop

Knowledge is not a substance. We cannot weigh biological knowledge against explanatory knowledge as though both occupied a common scale, or divide you into percentages of genes, culture, inexplicit knowledge, and explanation. The categories are useful because they make distinctions visible. They become dangerous when the distinctions harden into ingredients.

Not:

how much knowledge is there?

But:

what changed because this knowledge was present?

That question moves. Knowledge enters a problem situation and the situation is no longer the same. Something becomes possible. Something becomes impossible. A distinction appears; an old distinction disappears. A criticism becomes available. A problem becomes visible. The next state is not the previous state plus one more object. The relations have changed.

We've encountered this problem through the arrows between epistemic states of problems, tentative solutions, and criticism; through invention. A note changes you as you write it. A prototype changes what you can see in the problem. A criticism changes the abstraction being criticized. Objective knowledge returns to you and alters what you can do next.

We called it recursive and emergent. Both are useful. Both are dangerous. Either can make an explanation sound deeper without explaining anything. So the problem here is simple to state:

What does knowledge look like while it is moving?

1. Move One Word

Consider a sentence.

The dog bites the man.

Now move three words.

The man bites the dog.

Nothing supernatural happened. The same three nouns and verbs remain. The letters did not acquire new physical properties. No new vocabulary was introduced. Yet the meaning changed because the structure changed.

Grammar makes the relation unusually visible. The symbols do not carry the meaning independently. Their relations matter.

Try another.

Only she told him that I lied.

Move one word.

She only told him that I lied.

Move it again.

She told only him that I lied.

Again.

She told him only that I lied.

The physical change can be tiny while the semantic change is large. A few marks move across a page, their relation changes, and the sentence now says something different. That gives us a concrete place to begin. A system can contain substantially the same components while expressing something new because the relations among the components have changed.

It does not make relations magical. The relation is physically instantiated. A word occupies a different place. You process a different syntax. Different interpretations become available, and different consequences can follow. If the sentence is an instruction, different actions may occur. If it is a confession, different people may be blamed. If it is a law, different conduct may become prohibited.

Meaning has causal consequences because the structure carrying the meaning changed. This is ordinary, which gives us a useful constraint. When something genuinely new appears, we do not need to imagine that a new substance entered reality. Sometimes the novelty lies in the organization.

Now consider a chair.

Remove one leg. The chair is different and probably worse at being a chair. The difference is real, but difference alone is not creation in the sense that has concerned this inquiry. You changed the object. You did not necessarily create a new problem-solving structure.

Remove the back. Perhaps the remainder works as a small table. Again, structure and function have changed, but a flat surface elevated from the floor was already available as an abstraction. You have repurposed the object.

Now flip the original chair upside down. Put it in a gallery. Give it a title.

The Tyranny of Sitting.

Perhaps this is terrible art. That is not presently the problem.

Something different has happened. The chair still consists of wood, fasteners, joints, surfaces, and empty space, but it has entered another structure. Its orientation and context changed. Its title changed. Its relation to the viewer, to other objects, to inherited artistic conventions, and to the ordinary function of chairs changed.

The object can now participate in a criticism. Someone may laugh. Someone may be irritated. Someone may produce an essay about furniture and power. Someone else may produce a better artwork in response. The chair did not contain these consequences as hidden material properties waiting to be released.

The new organization changed what the object could mean and do inside an epistemic mind. Nothing magical occurred. The chair moved. The gallery admitted it. Someone named it. Someone encountered it. Relations formed. The resulting structure became capable of participating in problems that the chair in the dining room did not.

Known components. Inherited tools. Existing knowledge. A new relation. The inventory does not explain the result.

But neither does the phrase:

something emerged.

That word is often used exactly where an explanation becomes difficult. Cells interact; consciousness emerges. Individuals interact; society emerges. Neurons interact; meaning emerges. Simple rules interact; complexity emerges.

Sometimes this is harmless shorthand. Sometimes it is where explanation stops. We describe the components, describe the result, and put emergence between them:

A+B+C→emergence→D.A + B + C \rightarrow \text{emergence} \rightarrow D.A+B+C→emergence→D.

The mystery has not been solved. It has been labeled.

That would be especially damaging here. This inquiry has repeatedly refused to convert ignorance into ontology. We do not know how explanatory creativity works. Calling creativity emergent does not improve the situation. We do not know why a particular problem becomes salient. Calling salience emergent does not explain it. We do not know the causal path by which an inexplicit tension becomes an explicit criticism. Calling the transition emergent does not tell us what happened.

The useful part can be kept without treating the word as a cause. A new organization can appear through transformations among structures that already exist, and the new organization can possess causal or semantic properties that the components did not possess separately. We may call the appearance of that organization emergence.

But emergence is not cause. The transformations remain the explanatory problem.

Physical realism constrains the account. Whatever happened, happened in reality. No semantic spirit entered the sentence. No artistic essence entered the chair. No epistemic substance entered your brain. There were physical states, transformations among them, and relationships instantiated in those states.

If our explanation contains a gap, the gap remains ours. Reality did not skip the middle merely because our account did.

Incrementalism helps here. Earlier essays used it in social and technological contexts: changes can be local enough that their consequences remain criticizable and errors remain detectable. The same idea protects novelty from mystification.

Abstract it.

Suppose a new explanation exists at t2t_2t2​ that did not exist at t1t_1t1​. That is genuinely interesting. But it does not follow that reality jumped discontinuously between the states. There may be a path:

S1→S2→S3→⋯→Sn.S_1 \rightarrow S_2 \rightarrow S_3 \rightarrow \cdots \rightarrow S_n.S1​→S2​→S3​→⋯→Sn​.

Every local transformation can be physically ordinary. A memory becomes active. A sentence is reread. A distinction changes. A contradiction becomes salient. A word is written, and the written word alters the problem. Another explanation becomes relevant. A criterion changes. You sleep. The problem remains. You return. A new relation becomes explicit.

At the end, you say:

I have an idea.

The idea may be genuinely new. Nothing requires it to have existed as a hidden complete object at the beginning, and nothing requires a magical event at the end. The problem is to explain the transformations.

Often we cannot. The arrows between epistemic states are abstractions. They represent movement we have good reason to believe occurs. They do not claim to identify the neural machinery by which one thought becomes another.

Emergence does not close the arrows. It says that the resulting organization cannot be identified merely by listing the components that preceded it. That is useful. It is not an explanation of creativity.

The same pressure falls on compression. A map preserves selected relations while discarding enormous amounts of detail. A personality trait compresses many observations into a dimension. A model makes a system tractable by omitting structure. Compression helps explain why abstractions are powerful and why they can hide what matters.

But compression is not the whole story. We did not create the new sentence meaning by merely removing information. We rearranged the structure. We did not create the artistic claim by merely subtracting pieces of chair. We placed the object in new relations. An inventor may use parts that all existed before, while the invention is not explained by the inventory.

Separate those two operations. One removes structure while preserving what matters for a problem. The other reorganizes structure so that a relation becomes visible, objective, or operational in a form that was not previously available.

They can combine. A new abstraction may discard enormous detail while exposing a relation nobody had previously made explicit. The equation can be shorter than the observations while explaining more. The criticism can collapse a thousand pages into one distinction. Compression may participate, but the novelty lies in what the new organization can now do.

That gives us another constraint:

difference≠novel knowledge.\text{difference} \neq \text{novel knowledge}.difference=novel knowledge.

Randomly shuffle the words in a sentence and you will produce novelty in the trivial sense that the arrangement did not previously exist. Most arrangements will be nonsense. Break every leg off the chair and the resulting object is new in one physical sense. That does not make it an invention.

The epistemically interesting case is when a new organization changes the problem situation. A new criticism becomes possible. A new action, question, transformation, or relation becomes available. Something enters the causal world of knowledge that was not there before.

2. Evolution Is a Verb

This brings us back to evolution. The word is easy to hear as a noun: a topic in biology, a theory of speciation, a historical process that happened over immense periods before you were born. That is all true. It is also a compression.

The deeper structure is active. Variation occurs. Some variants persist and others disappear. What persists changes the starting conditions under which later variation occurs. The cycle continues. Evolution is not merely the history of biological change. It is change acting upon the conditions of later change.

Biological knowledge makes this unusually concrete. A mutation changes a structure. The structure changes the organism. The changed organism encounters the world differently. Its survival and reproduction occur under those conditions. Retained structures become part of the starting point from which later variants arise. The output returns as a condition of the next transformation.

Abstract it.

We can write a crude version:

V1→S1→R1.V_1 \rightarrow S_1 \rightarrow R_1.V1​→S1​→R1​.

Variation. Selection. Retention.

But retention is not an endpoint. The retained structure becomes part of the next state:

R1+V2→S2→R2.R_1 + V_2 \rightarrow S_2 \rightarrow R_2.R1​+V2​→S2​→R2​.

Now the recursive structure is visible. The result of one pass participates in the conditions of the next.

Nothing requires foresight. The selection mechanism need not understand what it retains. An eye can become better at forming useful images without any organism possessing an explanation of optics. A wing can become better suited to flight without any organism understanding aerodynamics. Biological knowledge accumulates without explanatory criticism. That is one of evolution's great explanatory achievements.

Now turn the structure toward epistemology as an explanation of knowledge. You also vary. Conjectures appear. Actions are tried. Explanations are criticized. Some structures persist and others disappear. What persists changes what you can do next. The resemblance to biological evolution matters, but it can hide the most important difference.

Consider the biological sequence:

variation→selection→retention→changed conditions for later variation.\text{variation} \rightarrow \text{selection} \rightarrow \text{retention} \rightarrow \text{changed conditions for later variation}.variation→selection→retention→changed conditions for later variation.

Now consider an epistemic sequence:

P1→TS1→EE1→P2.P_1 \rightarrow TS_1 \rightarrow EE_1 \rightarrow P_2.P1​→TS1​→EE1​→P2​.

Problem. Tentative solution. Error elimination. Successor problem. Here too, what results becomes part of what happens next. But epistemic selection can do something biological selection does not need to do: it can become a problem for itself.

You propose an explanation. Criticism exposes an error. The explanation changes. That is familiar. But the criticism can also expose an error in the criterion by which you were judging the explanation.

You can say:

I was asking the wrong question.

Or:

I thought this counted as evidence, but it does not.

Or:

I was evaluating the solution by a criterion that only made sense inside the old problem.

The process has not merely selected among variants. It has altered something about selection itself.

EEn→change in the conditions governing EEn+1.EE_n \rightarrow \text{change in the conditions governing } EE_{n+1}.EEn​→change in the conditions governing EEn+1​.

You can criticize the criteria of criticism. You can make the problem itself problematic. You can reject an inherited evaluative standard. You can construct a new distinction that changes which variants are even visible as variants. This does not make epistemic evolution supernatural. It makes the selection mechanism different and presently mysterious.

Biological selection can accumulate knowledge without knowing that selection is occurring. Epistemic selection can produce explanations about selection. That explanation can become objective knowledge, and objective knowledge can alter later selection. The process turns upon itself.

3. The Old Problem Is Not the New Problem

Now recursion has earned its name. A recursive epistemic process is not merely a loop:

A→B→A.A \rightarrow B \rightarrow A.A→B→A.

That would be repetition. The interesting structure is:

A1→B1A_1 \rightarrow B_1A1​→B1​

followed by:

(A1+B1)→A2.(A_1 + B_1) \rightarrow A_2.(A1​+B1​)→A2​.

The output returns, but because the output exists, the next input situation is different. An inventor makes a prototype. The prototype fails. The failure becomes objective knowledge. The inventor encounters that knowledge and is now different. The next prototype is created from a problem situation that includes the failure of the first.

The object changes the inventor. The changed inventor changes the object.

M0→O1→EE1→M1→O2.M_0 \rightarrow O_1 \rightarrow EE_1 \rightarrow M_1 \rightarrow O_2.M0​→O1​→EE1​→M1​→O2​.

This structure makes invention concrete. The deeper point is that the output does not merely come back. It returns to a system that has already been changed by producing it. The loop changes the thing that loops.

Now let the thing that loops be you. You are stuck. A problem has occupied you for days. Perhaps you are writing, designing, trying to understand a conflict, debugging a program, or making a decision. Something does not fit. You think about it and nothing happens. So you leave. You read something unrelated. You have a conversation. You sleep. You walk. You become interested in something else. Then you return, and the problem looks different.

We often say:

I needed time to think.

Or:

I had to let it gestate.

Or:

the answer came to me later.

Those phrases are useful, but they can hide the process. The problem did not necessarily sit unchanged inside a sealed compartment until an answer appeared.

You changed. Knowledge entered. Other knowledge disappeared from attention. Memories shifted in salience. Other problems became active. New relations formed. Inexplicit knowledge may have changed before any new explicit sentence appeared. The old problem then encountered a different epistemic system.

Abstract it again.

We can represent the movement crudely:

(P0,K0)→K1.(P_0, K_0) \rightarrow K_1.(P0​,K0​)→K1​.

Then:

(P0,K1)→P1.(P_0, K_1) \rightarrow P_1.(P0​,K1​)→P1​.

Then:

(P1,K1)→K2.(P_1, K_1) \rightarrow K_2.(P1​,K1​)→K2​.

The notation is not your mind. It only prevents us from imagining repetition where change is occurring.

You say:

I kept thinking about the same problem.

Perhaps. But the problem at the tenth encounter may no longer be the problem at the first. One assumption has become visible. One memory now matters. One distinction has disappeared. One criticism has acquired force. One criterion no longer feels compelling. The language remains the same. The epistemic object has moved.

This does not make every repeated thought productive. A fear can recur. A grievance can become more rigid. An obsession can narrow the available problem space. A circular explanation can immunize itself against criticism. Recursion is not necessarily good. Evolution is not necessarily progress. Emergence is not necessarily improvement.

The question remains:

what is changing?

A repeated thought can return to a nearly identical structure and strengthen it, or it can re-enter a changing system and acquire a new relation. From outside, the difference may be difficult to see.

One process repeats:

P→TS→P→TS→P.P \rightarrow TS \rightarrow P \rightarrow TS \rightarrow P.P→TS→P→TS→P.

Another moves:

P1→TS1→EE1→P2→TS2→EE2→P3.P_1 \rightarrow TS_1 \rightarrow EE_1 \rightarrow P_2 \rightarrow TS_2 \rightarrow EE_2 \rightarrow P_3.P1​→TS1​→EE1​→P2​→TS2​→EE2​→P3​.

Both may look stuck. Both may talk about the same subject and return to the same memory. Inside, one structure may be hardening while the other is changing. The observable category does not tell us which. The arrows can change the conditions under which later arrows appear. The landscape is landscaping itself.

That makes another familiar question look strange: what is the essence of a person? It cannot be biological, psychological, or social essence. You are not the Big Five, a diagnosis, a demographic category, a genome, a role, or a fixed collection of values.

Essentialism cannot be rescued by becoming more sophisticated. Perhaps your "essence" is massive a compression of your exceptional epistemic mind. Perhaps you are the kind of entity that instantiates:

P1→TS→EE→P2.P_1 \rightarrow TS \rightarrow EE \rightarrow P_2.P1​→TS→EE→P2​.

We have found a deep structure. But what have we actually explained?

Knowing the structure of a process does not tell us what happens when the process is turned on. You can instantiate the same abstract schema as another person while beginning with different objective knowledge, memories, bodies, inexplicit expectations, problems, criticisms, and criteria. The first transition changes the conditions of the second. The second changes the conditions of the third. The trajectories can diverge radically and rapidly.

A structural account may be true and still fail completely as an explanation of its content. That is not a defect in the structure. It is one of its consequences. A recursive process cannot be understood merely by freezing the process into a noun. You are not an epistemic cycle stored in a body. You are the cycle moving through an evolving and historically particular world while changing the world through which you move.

Even the compression, universal explainer, can become misleading if read as an essence. The phrase does not identify a hidden substance shared by people. It identifies a capacity, and a capacity in motion produces trajectories whose contents do not yet exist.

Your future explanation cannot be extracted from the definition of the explainer. If it could, it would not be new.

4. Objective Knowledge Enters the Loop

Your recursive epistemic movement does not occur alone. You live among objective knowledge: books, interfaces, stories, laws, scientific theories, images, algorithms, schools, tools, other people, artificial intelligences. These structures do not merely supply information to an otherwise unchanged you. They can alter the conditions under which you create knowledge.

Writing changed what could be remembered. Notation changed what could be calculated. Scientific instruments changed what could be observed. Software changed what could be searched and simulated. Artificial intelligence changes what can be generated, compared, criticized, preserved, and returned.

Objective knowledge becomes part of the recursive environment. An external artifact enters. You change. The changed you creates or selects another artifact. That artifact returns. The process continues.

This is why technological arguments framed only as exposure can become misleading. How many hours? How many messages? How many videos? How many prompts? How much screen time? The measurements may be useful, but they observe the outer surface of a recursively changing relationship. The same artifact can become distraction, instruction, friendship, obsession, a tool, a criticism, a new problem, or a way out.

The external object does not uniquely specify what is happening inside your epistemic mind. That does not make technological effects unreal. It makes the explanatory problem harder.

The same difficulty appears in the language of technological lock-in. A child is locked into a platform. A population is locked into an algorithm. A worker is locked into an interface. You are locked into a media environment. The image is spatial: a prison, walls, someone inside. But what exactly is locked? Behavior? Attention? A social network? A category? A preference? A memory? A habit? A problem situation? A selection criterion? A set of available conjectures?

Once those possibilities are separated, possible mechanisms become visible. A system can constrain exit. It can make alternatives expensive. It can hide available options. It can repeatedly return one class of objective knowledge while suppressing another. It can force you to represent yourself through fixed categories. It can reward one kind of expression and punish another. It can make criticism difficult to discover or costly to express.

But an active epistemic subject complicates the prison. You can notice the walls and criticize them. You can repurpose a tool, use one part of a platform to learn how another works, or build a new community inside a structure designed for another purpose. You can leave. You can fail to leave. You can want to stay. You can create a problem nobody who designed the system anticipated.

The prison may still be real, but we cannot infer it merely from the shape of the technology. We have to explain which epistemic transformations have become unavailable, costly, or invisible. That is a stranger prison. You may participate in constructing the loop that constrains you, while also being the entity within it capable of making the loop itself a problem.

That changes the moral question, if you choose it. Earlier parts derived a familiar principle: preserve the means of criticism. Freedom of speech, association, conscience, exit, institutional openness, and the ability to change your mind preserve conditions under which error correction can continue.

But the moving epistemic subject adds a complication. You cannot exercise a criticism that does not yet exist for you. You cannot leave by a path you cannot yet imagine. You cannot reject a category that has not yet become problematic. You cannot formulate the alternative problem that has not yet become visible.

The formal permission to criticize may be present while the criticism is absent. That does not justify giving an authority power to manufacture the correct criticism on your behalf. The authority also lacks the uncreated knowledge.

The moral problem is therefore deeper: what conditions leave enough openness for new criticism to become possible without presuming that anyone can know in advance what the criticism will be?

The word emerge must remain disciplined here. A new criticism does not appear from nowhere. It may grow through conversation, failure, boredom, exposure to an alien explanation, an inexplicit discomfort, a mistake, a technology, or a child asking the wrong question. The conditions matter. But no inventory of the conditions contains the criticism as a predetermined conclusion.

You can become able to think something you could not think before. That is the moral significance of your moving epistemic mind.

5. When the Representation Returns

The same structure becomes technological once a system represents you and returns that representation to the environment in which you continue to act. A static model says: the user has preferences; the student has a level; the patient has a profile; the worker has a role; the customer has a segment; the child has a measured ability. The system learns the state and serves the state back.

This can work. Recommendation, personalization, classification, and stable interfaces are useful because you cannot reconstruct every problem from first principles at every moment.

But recursion introduces a different question. What happens when the representation re-enters the process that produced it?

Abstract it.

A system classifies you. The classification changes what you see. What you see changes what you do. What you do becomes new data. The new data strengthens the classification.

C1→E1→B1→C2.C_1 \rightarrow E_1 \rightarrow B_1 \rightarrow C_2.C1​→E1​→B1​→C2​.

Classification. Environment. Behavior. Updated classification.

The loop is not automatically bad. A language tutor can adapt to a learner and improve instruction. A medical device can adapt to changing physiology. A creative tool can remember a project and return relevant material.

The question is what can still change inside the loop. Can you surprise the model? Can the category be reopened? Can the problem be reformulated? Can the history be inspected? Can an earlier state return? Can the system preserve contradiction rather than averaging it away? Can you reject the representation? Can the system support a transition that the previous model of you would never have predicted?

A faithful epistemic technology cannot merely preserve what you know. It must avoid making your previous state into a cage for your next one.

Computation makes the possibility unusually concrete because representations can be stored, copied, compared, simulated, recombined, and returned. A computational system can preserve states with a fidelity biological memory cannot.

Imagine working on a problem with an external system. The system does not merely store the latest answer. It preserves:

P1,TS1,EE1,P2,TS2,EE2.P_1, TS_1, EE_1, P_2, TS_2, EE_2.P1​,TS1​,EE1​,P2​,TS2​,EE2​.

You can return to an abandoned conjecture or compare an old criterion with a new one. You can recover the point at which the problem changed, notice that a criticism now matters that previously did not, or ask what disappeared from the current representation. The technology can make movement objective. It does not create the movement. It gives movement a surface.

Invention reveals something similar. A sketch preserves enough of an earlier epistemic state for the changed inventor to encounter it again. The old artifact becomes new input. Computation can expand this capacity dramatically.

The danger is symmetrical. A system that preserves movement can also freeze it. It can compress you into one profile or treat the latest state as the true state. It can predict the next problem from the previous ones and quietly suppress what falls outside the prediction. It can interpret inconsistency as noise instead of potential criticism.

Then the technology does not merely represent you badly. Its representation can become causal. You begin to move through a world partially structured by the system's compressed account of who you were. The abstraction returns. The loop closes.

Now widen the loop once more. A new explanation is created. At first it exists only in one person. Then it is written and becomes objective knowledge. Someone else reads it; their problem changes. They build a tool. The tool changes what other people can do. An institution adopts the tool and changes its procedures. Children grow up inside those procedures. For them, the structure may no longer appear as an invention. It appears as the environment.

The organization that emerged at one point has become a condition of later variation. That is exactly what evolution teaches us to expect: retained knowledge alters the environment in which later knowledge develops.

Epistemic evolution adds another turn. A later person can reconstruct the history. You can inherit such a structure and ask why it is here and what problem it was solving. You can ask whether that problem still exists, what the structure made possible, what it suppressed, and what happens if it changes. The environment can become an object of explanation. The inherited can become a problem. The process recurses again.

This is why the repeated contrast between nouns and verbs matters. Person. Knowledge. Culture. Intelligence. Education. Technology. Evolution. Mind. Each noun is useful because it gives us a handle. Each can also freeze movement.

Evolution is especially revealing. Heard as a noun, it sounds like an object of knowledge: a scientific subject, a chapter in a textbook. Heard as a process, it is retained structure altering the conditions of later variation.

Knowledge does something similar. A noun suggests possession. Knowing still sounds like a state. But the movement is richer: problems become conjectures; conjectures become criticisms; criticisms become new problems. Inexplicit knowledge becomes explicit. Explicit knowledge becomes objective. Objective knowledge returns and changes inexplicit expectations. Biological knowledge builds a person capable of criticizing biological explanations. An institution embodies an old solution until someone makes the institution itself a problem.

The categories remain useful. Reality keeps moving across their boundaries. The movement is primary. The nouns are our handles.

6. But the Children

Have you heard? Children are getting dumber. This time we can measure it.

You've heard some version of the story. International tests, teenagers across dozens of countries, declining scores in mathematics, reading, science. Reading is especially worrying: not just recognizing words, but connecting sources, evaluating claims, thinking critically. Exactly what everyone says matters in the age of AI.

Meanwhile, students use chatbots for homework. Phones distract classrooms. Children read less, struggle to concentrate, and let machines answer before learning to remain inside a question.

The decline began before AI, but that detail fades as the story travels. Scores down. Screens everywhere. Attention collapsing. Machines doing the thinking.

The pieces fit together. The explanation feels obvious.

We built extraordinary machines. Then we put them in the hands of children. Now the measurements are arriving. And the books, and the panic, and the policies.

The tempting move is obvious: put the technology on one side, the test scores on the other, and call the relation an explanation. The knowledge of knowledge in motion makes that move harder. Much harder.

A changed outcome is not yet a causal path. Exposure is not yet an epistemic trajectory. A category is not yet the movement inside it. If the children have changed, what changed in the recursive conditions under which they learn, criticize, remember, attend, conjecture, and return to problems?

The truck is hurdling toward us. There is a brick on the accelerator. It is full of children.

Do you really want to stop it and inspect what's inside?
