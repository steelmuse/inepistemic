Acknowledgements

"I am not a theoretician, and I don’t pretend to be a savant." — Thomas Alva Edison

I am an inventor by trade and temperament; software is the substrate. That matters here. The architecture for this project came before the book. The technical environment developed for the project is called Explanata, harnessed to GPT-5.6 Sol for the book's production. I operated the inquiry conversationally, posing problems, criticizing outputs, introducing distinctions, rejecting formulations, and deciding which successor problems were worth pursuing; the manuscript emerged from that process.

The underlying effort has been to treat epistemology as a first-class architectural abstraction: to ask what happens when aspects of epistemology are made explicit enough to be modeled, implemented, and processed. Software provides an environment where those abstractions can be explored. This book is one of many things that emerged when the resulting architecture was turned back upon the architect and the epistemology itself.

For that reason, the people I acknowledge as entrepreneurs, inventors, and engineers stand here as peers of those I acknowledge for their epistemic contributions. I have spent much of my career building things with other people. Problems found in companies, products, systems, interfaces, failed designs, working designs, arguments, and collaborations have shaped this inquiry as surely as books have. Many colleagues taught me that explanations are easy until something has to ship.

Steven Pinker, especially through The Blank Slate, How the Mind Works, and The Stuff of Thought, helped structure the cognitive-scientific and psychological landscape through which some of those problems became concrete. Scott Aaronson, through his explorations of computational complexity, quantum mechanics, and the nature of minds, sharpened questions at the intersection of computation, epistemology, and personhood. Richard Dawkins supplied evolutionary concepts and language that recur throughout the work, including the wonderfully dismissive phrase “Popperian heckle,” which seemed worth taking seriously.

Karl Popper and David Deutsch remain in the foreground of this project, but not in a straight line. I encountered Popper more deeply through Deutsch. Deutsch first made explanations themselves into a problem for me: not merely things philosophy talks about, but structures whose reach, consilience, constraints, and consequences could be investigated. That inquiry eventually led me further into Popper—into problems, conjecture and criticism, objective knowledge, fallibilism, and an epistemology much deeper than the familiar caricature of falsificationism.

Misunderstanding is catalytic. Explanations of explanation carried me through a wider philosophical landscape: Hempel, Kitcher, van Fraassen, Cartwright, Woodward, Strevens, and others working on causation, unification, models, realism, inference, probability, and what makes an explanation explanatory at all. Much of that work now stands for me more as a constructive foil. A parallel lineage also shaped my understanding of these problems: Miller, Bronowski, Lakatos, Shearmur, Bartley, Agassi, Corvi, Jarvie, Maxwell, Musgrave, and many others each deepened my understanding of Popper and the critical-rationalist tradition from different directions. Competing formulations exposed hidden assumptions, supplied counterexamples, and made the points of agreement and disagreement with Popper sharper.

The Conjecture Institute helped consolidate and sustain an intellectual environment in which these questions could remain live rather than settle into doctrine. Brett Hall has been a public teacher of record over many years, particularly through his podcasts and conversations about Deutsch, Popper, explanation, and the reach of ideas. His The Farthest Reaches occupies territory close enough to this work that the parallel deserves recognition, one pointing the person outward, the other inward. Ray Scott Percival’s The Myth of the Closed Mind occupies nearby Popperian territory, particularly in its treatment of human rationality, corrigibility, and the possibility of an open mind. Aaron Stupple's The Sovereign Child sharpened problems around children, authority, and epistemic sovereignty, and our conversations carried those problems beyond the book.

The movement was not from one authority to another. The inventive process navigated a landscape of competing abstractions, implemented some of them, collided them with one another, and retained what seemed to work. Deutsch has described his own work, in effect, as footnotes to Popper. I am comfortable placing this project in that lineage, though a builder needs a different metaphor: call them prototypes. I have inherited problems, explanations, vocabulary, and an epistemic attitude from them, and those ideas mutated inside problems they did not set for me. Where that process has produced errors, the errors are mine.

None of those acknowledgements should be read as warrants. The essay titles were constructed similarly: inherited memes, mechanically mutated to fit new problems. Their ancestry is part of the mechanism, not an assertion that the essays respond to the works whose language they inherit. A reference may identify ancestry, resemblance, disagreement, mutation, or simply another place where a problem has been encountered. I have therefore resisted using citations and parenthetical references throughout the manuscript as placeholders for explanations.

To everyone who built, argued, explained, criticized, listened, invented, funded, programmed, published, conversed, or otherwise altered the problem situation from which this work emerged: thank you. What survives here is one reconstruction among many possibilities, for your criticism.
