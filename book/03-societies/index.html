The Solitary Explainer

III. The Anxious Authorities

At the social level, the problem of you changes because society is not merely a distant influence. It supplies much of the material with which you think: language, rules, roles, expectations, explanations, institutions, laws, stories, technologies, and inherited ways of framing problems. Call it social objective knowledge, or social knowledge.

Social knowledge can therefore act on you far more directly than a statistical relation between genetic variation and behavior. That makes the social case both stronger and more dangerous.

The error is not that society has little causal effect. The error appears when knowledge about a social regularity is treated as knowledge of the kind of person who participates in it.

Abstract that idea.

The conventional movement is familiar:

group regularity→group property→individual explanation.\text{group regularity} \rightarrow \text{group property} \rightarrow \text{individual explanation}.group regularity→group property→individual explanation.

A bureaucracy exists. A legal system exists. A language exists. A political doctrine exists. The social object can be real, and the regularity measured across it can also be real. But the reality of the object does not make the category naming it an explanation of the individual who encounters it.

Locate the pattern in politics.

A political survey makes the distinction unusually clear. Suppose two people both endorse proposition QQQ:

P1→QP_1 \rightarrow QP1​→Q

and

P2→Q.P_2 \rightarrow Q.P2​→Q.

The survey may have discovered something straightforward: both people presently endorse the same proposition. What it has not discovered is why either person endorses it. One may have reached it through moral reasoning, another through personal experience, economic argument, trust in an authority, or failure to consider an alternative.

Compare it.

The important relation is:

same expressed knowledge≠same explanatory history.\text{same expressed knowledge} \neq \text{same explanatory history}.same expressed knowledge=same explanatory history.

The group description can represent something real while leaving the person unexplained.

The social case goes further because many social objects are themselves shareable structures that survive any one person's participation in them. A language can be learned by different speakers. A constitution can be read, criticized, amended, obeyed, or ignored by different citizens. A professional code can constrain people who disagree about its justification. A moral tradition can be inherited before you can state the principles it contains.

Call these publicly available structures objective knowledge: knowledge embodied in forms that can be evaluated across solitary minds. You do not begin by inventing the language, law, institution, or tradition. You encounter it already there.

That encounter changes what becomes thinkable and actionable. A rule can close an option. A norm can make one action intelligible and another shameful. A hierarchy can attach sanctions to disagreement. A doctrine can supply an explanation before you have encountered a criticism of it.

A gene cannot tell a child that adults know better. A culture can. A gene cannot tell an employee that managers are entitled to decide. An institution can. A gene cannot tell a citizen that obedience to authority is virtuous. A political tradition can.

Society therefore does not simply surround you. It helps constitute the field of problems, expectations, explanations, and constraints within which you act.

Call that surrounding structure your problem situation: the problems you can see, the knowledge you have inherited, the options you believe are available, the sanctions you anticipate, the explanations you can presently use, and the criticisms you can presently formulate.

If social knowledge enters your problem situation, what explains your action?

Take a bureaucrat. At work, the person may be formal, procedural, risk-averse, deferential to hierarchy, and emotionally restrained. At home, the same person may be playful, spontaneous, irreverent, affectionate, and improvisational.

Labels such as role, organizational culture, situational norm, or identity activation can mark real regularities. But they do not yet tell us what this person understands themselves to be doing.

The person may understand the workplace as a system in which procedure defines admissible action, disagreement carries career costs, success means avoiding unauthorized risk, and superiors possess legitimate decision rights. At home, those problems may disappear. Other obligations, possibilities, and expectations become active.

Abstract that structure.

The causal structure is closer to:

socially inherited knowledge+individual problem situation→interpretation→action.\text{socially inherited knowledge} + \text{individual problem situation} \\ \rightarrow \text{interpretation} \rightarrow \text{action}.socially inherited knowledge+individual problem situation→interpretation→action.

The same mind can behave differently because different knowledge and different problems become active in different circumstances. No contradiction in personality is required.

This does not make the institutional regularity unreal. If an organization gives many people similar procedures, incentives, sanctions, vocabularies, and decision rights, similar behavior is unsurprising. The regularity may explain something important about the institution. The point is narrower: the causal route into action still passes through a person who interprets what those conditions mean.

Nor is interpretation unconstrained. Institutions can alter the actual possibility space around you. Law changes which actions are permitted. Censorship changes which criticism can circulate. Employment hierarchy changes who possesses formal decision rights.

Social conditions can also remove conditions needed for the normal exercise of explanatory agency. Solitary confinement, for example, can remove ordinary social interaction and contribute to psychological deterioration. That does not make the mind a lesser kind of mind. It shows that explanatory agency has enabling conditions: a universal capacity can be impaired when conditions required for its exercise are removed.

Compare it.

The distinction is therefore not between social causation and individual freedom. It is between two different explanatory claims:

restricted action space≠restricted explanatory capacity.\text{restricted action space} \neq \text{restricted explanatory capacity}.restricted action space=restricted explanatory capacity.

You may act cautiously because criticism is punished. An employee may defer because formal authority is backed by sanctions. A bureaucrat may follow procedure because procedure determines what counts as an admissible move. Those conditions can explain recurring behavior without becoming an intrinsic property of the people who behave that way.

This is where social essentialism enters.

Stop. There's no such thing as an essence. Reverse the definition.

Behavior produced under conditions is redescribed as a property of the people who inhabit them: bureaucrats are rigid, members of hierarchical societies are deferential, children are suggestible.

Compress it.

The observation may summarize a distribution. The explanatory inflation occurs when:

behavior under conditions\text{behavior under conditions}behavior under conditions

becomes

property of person.\text{property of person}.property of person.

A social category can tell us which roles exist, what sanctions are likely, which propositions are widely shared, or which actions are formally possible. It does not by itself tell us which of those things this person accepts, fears, misunderstands, criticizes, combines, or rejects. That requires your explanatory history.

Much of that history need not be explicit. A child can learn who is allowed to interrupt whom before possessing a theory of hierarchy. An employee can learn which disagreements are career-limiting without formulating a doctrine of managerial authority. A student can learn which questions count as challenges before being able to describe the rule being enforced.

Because this is knowledge rather than essence, it can conflict with other knowledge. You can retain one rule in one domain and reject it in another. An inherited norm can remain invisible until criticism exposes it. An explanation can be revised without you becoming a different kind of mind.

Knowledge learned this way can remain inexplicit: operative in action before you can state the rule being followed.

You are therefore not merely the endpoint of social causation. You are where inherited social knowledge is interpreted, combined, criticized, and sometimes changed.

Authority makes the structure especially visible.

A persistent social pattern can be understood as a network of propositions, norms, habits, and institutional arrangements concerning who is entitled to decide for whom. It need not be a collective mind or a hidden social trait. Its content may be learned through recurring relations: parents over children, teachers over students, managers over employees, experts over non-experts, rulers over citizens.

These relations do not merely recur independently. They can persist as inherited knowledge, reproduced through explicit propositions, inexplicit habits, recurring practices, and institutions. Call such a transmissible unit of social knowledge a meme. Where several such memes support and reproduce one another, they form a memeplex. The recurring knowledge concerning who is entitled to decide for whom can therefore be treated as an authority memeplex.

Abstract it again.

The relation can become familiar before anyone states it as a doctrine:

higher-status actor→greater decision authority.\text{higher-status actor} \rightarrow \text{greater decision authority}.higher-status actor→greater decision authority.

Modern liberal societies also inherit a newer memeplex organized around autonomy, equality, freedom of speech, democratic participation, freedom of conscience, informed consent, and individual rights. This newer knowledge can coexist with the older authority memeplex inside the same society and inside the same person. The newer vocabulary can therefore sit on top of older authority relations without fully replacing them. The older structure may reappear when applying the newer principle would dissolve an established authority relation.

One relation says:

person→presumptive autonomy.\text{person} \rightarrow \text{presumptive autonomy}.person→presumptive autonomy.

Another says:

authority differential→legitimate control.\text{authority differential} \rightarrow \text{legitimate control}.authority differential→legitimate control.

You may defend freedom of judgment in politics while treating obedience as obvious at work. An employee may resent managerial control and reproduce the same authority relation with a child. A professional may affirm autonomy while regarding expertise as sufficient grounds to override another person's interpretation.

A hidden trait called authoritarianism is not required to make those conflicts intelligible. Partially incompatible bodies of inherited knowledge may become active in different problem situations.

The personal question is then, which knowledge is active here, what problem does it appear to solve, and what criticism could expose the conflict?

Children put unusual pressure on this question because differences in knowledge and practical competence are obvious.

The familiar argument is:

children know less, therefore adults should decide for them.

The first proposition is often true. The second introduces a stronger relation:

less knowledge→less epistemic sovereignty.\text{less knowledge} \rightarrow \text{less epistemic sovereignty}.less knowledge→less epistemic sovereignty.

Once stated in that form, the principle is not logically confined to childhood. It can be extended wherever one person or institution judges another to know less: patients, students, employees, voters, or populations regarded as vulnerable to misinformation.

When differences in knowledge are used to justify differences in your authority over your own judgment, the result is a doctrine of graded epistemic personhood.

The child case is relatively easy because unequal knowledge is visible. But unequal knowledge and unequal kinds of mind are not the same claim.

A pilot may know more than a passenger about landing an aircraft. A doctor may know more than a patient about a disease. An adult may know more than a child about traffic. These differences concern knowledge possessed in a domain. They do not establish a different capacity for creating knowledge.

Abstract it.

The distinction is:

knowledge possessed\text{knowledge possessed}knowledge possessed

versus

capacity for knowledge creation.\text{capacity for knowledge creation}.capacity for knowledge creation.

A child begins with less knowledge, fewer experiences, fewer available explanations, and less practical control over circumstances. Those are real features of the child's problem situation. Yet the child still has to interpret what happens. The child forms expectations, notices anomalies, invents explanations, imitates, asks questions, misunderstands, tests, criticizes, and revises.

Authority can therefore change more than what the child is allowed to do. It can change which problems the child is permitted to pursue, which explanations are available, and which criticisms survive long enough to develop.

Media makes that change easy to see.

One common model treats the child as a passive recipient. The medium acts; the child absorbs. Its causal structure is:

content→mind.\text{content} \rightarrow \text{mind}.content→mind.

We will call this the bucket theory of mind. Once the child is pictured as a container, controlling inputs looks like a direct way of controlling what enters the mind.

But the child cannot receive a medium without interpreting it. A child looking at a screen must conjecture what is happening, what matters, what another person intends, what will happen next, and which rules govern the situation.

Abstract it again.

The structure is closer to:

media environment→problems encountered→child’s conjectures and criticism.\text{media environment} \rightarrow \text{problems encountered} \rightarrow \text{child's conjectures and criticism}.media environment→problems encountered→child’s conjectures and criticism.

Removing a screen therefore does not merely remove content. It may terminate an active problem environment selected by the child. A parent may imagine that "go outside and play" moves the child into a richer world. The child may experience the opposite: a move from a dense unresolved problem space into one containing nothing presently salient.

The physical medium does not settle that difference. The explanation depends on what problem this child is pursuing through it.

That same distinction helps with recurring concern about new media. Reading, radio, television, the internet, social media, and generative AI differ technically and can generate different harms. Any specific criticism may turn out to be correct. But as one medium becomes ordinary infrastructure, concern can migrate to the next. That recurrence does not show that the specific criticisms are false. It suggests that the changing technical criticism may not exhaust the explanation. Each medium also changes the routes by which you encounter knowledge and who or what controls your next epistemic encounter.

Social media changes association. A rare interest once constrained by geography can become a searchable community:

rare interest+geographic dispersion→searchable community.\text{rare interest} + \text{geographic dispersion} \rightarrow \text{searchable community}.rare interest+geographic dispersion→searchable community.

That same structure can support ideological reinforcement, specialist collaboration, support networks, or scientific exchange. "Polarization" can describe one outcome without exhausting the architectural change.

Let's introduce AI as a new medium and contrast it with older forms of media.

Generative AI changes something else. Social media helps you find others already organized around an interest. A search engine retrieves from an existing informational landscape. A generative system can recombine inherited knowledge around a problem that may not yet correspond to an existing document, course, community, or established query. It can therefore create a responsive interaction around your present problem:

individual problem→generative system→candidate explanation→criticism→new problem.\text{individual problem} \rightarrow \text{generative system} \\ \rightarrow \text{candidate explanation} \rightarrow \text{criticism} \rightarrow \text{new problem}.individual problem→generative system→candidate explanation→criticism→new problem.

Call it an application of epistemic processing.

For a child pursuing an unusual scientific, technical, philosophical, or artistic problem, the former bottleneck may have been the accidental availability of an adult or institution able to respond. A generative system can alter that bottleneck by making a candidate explanatory response immediately available.

That does not establish that the response is true, reliable, or worth retaining. It changes the problem situation. It changes what can be asked next and how quickly a candidate explanation can be produced, criticized, and replaced.

This is why opposite complaints about media need not identify one mechanism. Social media can be criticized for excessive differentiation; AI for excessive commonality; unusually capable AI for threatening human distinctiveness. Those claims concern different possible effects. What the media can share is a transfer of some control over the next epistemic encounter away from incumbent curators, away from the incumbent authorities.

And the authorities become anxious.

That transfer is not, by itself, a judgment about whether the change is good or bad. It identifies a change in the distribution of epistemic control.

Now return to the original social-scientific problem. This personal analysis changes its significance.

A theory about society can become part of society. A claim that children are vulnerable to media can alter parental behavior, school rules, platform policy, regulation, and public norms. Claims that workers require supervision, voters are irrational, social media polarizes, or AI causes dependence can likewise be built into institutions.

Once institutionalized, such claims cease to be mere descriptions. They become causal inputs into the lives of the people described.

Abstract it.

The sequence can be:

population observation→social category→policy interpretation→institutional rule→individual problem environment.\text{population observation} \rightarrow \text{social category} \rightarrow \\ \text{policy interpretation} \rightarrow \text{institutional rule} \rightarrow \text{individual problem environment}.population observation→social category→policy interpretation→institutional rule→individual problem environment.

Suppose an association between media use and distress in a population of children is redescribed as "children are vulnerable to media," and that description then supports rules controlling children's access. Whether the policy works is a separate empirical question. The epistemic transition has already occurred: a class-level observation has been translated into an authority relation over particular people.

The social sciences therefore contain a reflexive causal route:

theory of society→policy→social structure→individual problem environment.\text{theory of society} \rightarrow \text{policy} \rightarrow \text{social structure} \rightarrow \text{individual problem environment}.theory of society→policy→social structure→individual problem environment.

A weak explanatory category can become a strong institutional constraint.

That return also clarifies what the original social knowledge can genuinely tell us. Social categories can identify distributions, shared propositions, institutional rules, recurring behavior, authority structures, legal restrictions, communication networks, and changes in information environments. Some social structures provide direct causal explanations of constraints: a law prohibits an action, a hierarchy allocates a decision right, censorship removes a channel of criticism.

What the category does not contain merely by naming the group or regularity is the explanatory history of the individual.

The conventional movement tends to be:

group→regularity→social category→individual expectation→policy.\text{group} \rightarrow \text{regularity} \\ \rightarrow \text{social category} \rightarrow \text{individual expectation} \rightarrow \text{policy}.group→regularity→social category→individual expectation→policy.

The epistemic movement keeps the social knowledge but relocates you inside the causal chain:

socially inherited knowledge+individual problem situation→interpretation→criticism or retention→action.\text{socially inherited knowledge} + \text{individual problem situation} \\ \rightarrow \text{interpretation} \rightarrow \text{criticism or retention} \rightarrow \text{action}.socially inherited knowledge+individual problem situation→interpretation→criticism or retention→action.

Society changes the concepts available to you, the problems that become salient, the explanations encountered before criticism begins, the sanctions attached to dissent, the criticism that can circulate, and the actions that are possible. You may accept that inherited knowledge, misunderstand it, combine incompatible parts of it, reproduce it elsewhere, criticize it, or replace it with a new explanation.

That is how the same social knowledge can produce regularities without producing identical minds. It is also how you can participate in a regularity without being explained by the category that records it.

The deeper distinction is not simply between a group statistic and an individual case. It is between social knowledge and the explanatory history of the person who encounters that knowledge. The first can describe real institutions, propositions, constraints, and distributions outside you. The second explains how you encounter that world and what happens next.

Confusing them turns social knowledge into a substitute for you.

The alternative is not to imagine yourself outside society. Societal knowledge is close to the content of individual minds precisely because it is inherited through language, institutions, norms, laws, roles, media, technologies, and authority relations. It can constrain action, alter which criticism is available, and determine which problems become visible first.

Its causal power is why classificatory overreach matters. But none of those transitions requires a lesser kind of person beneath the constraint.

I feel an abstraction coming on.

The negative distinction remains:

social constraint⇏lesser explanatory person.\text{social constraint} \not\Rightarrow \text{lesser explanatory person}.social constraint⇒lesser explanatory person.

The positive relation is more important:

socially inherited knowledge↓person-specific problems↓possible criticism and new knowledge.\begin{gather*} \text{socially inherited knowledge} \\ \downarrow \\ \text{person-specific problems} \\ \downarrow \\ \text{possible criticism and new knowledge}. \end{gather*}socially inherited knowledge↓person-specific problems↓possible criticism and new knowledge.​

You can inherit norms of autonomy and hierarchy at the same time. A child can know less without being a lesser kind of knower. A medium can change a child's epistemic landscape without mechanically writing content into the mind. An institution can restrict action without defining the explanatory capacity of the person subject to it.

The social world enters you as a field of inherited explanations, constraints, and problems. It does not enter as a completed psychological essence.

There's no such thing as an essence. What's the next problem?

If social knowledge acts through individual problem situations, where is the society that appears to know, decide, and command?
