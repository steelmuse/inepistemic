The Solitary Explainer

IV. The Wisdom Between The Crowd

You remain the locus of explanation. Here, that commitment is applied to society itself.

Wait. That sounds like a contradiction. The individual is the locus of society?

The conventional language of social life is saturated with collective subjects: society believes; the government knows; the market decides; science says; the church teaches; the company wants; the public demands. These expressions are useful shorthand, but the shorthand can become an ontology.

Speak clearly.

A stable social pattern begins to sound like a subject that sees, judges, intends, and knows. There is no social mind in the explanation developed here. There are individual minds. Each person is a distinct locus of experience, conjecture, interpretation, criticism, and action. Each knows only part of what other people know and only part of the social world in which they act. Communication can connect these minds without merging them. Institutions can coordinate them without creating a superordinate knower.

Explain.

What persists between people is not a shared subjectivity but knowledge made objective. Call it objective knowledge. Explanations, rules, classifications, procedures, records, software, texts, and standards can outlast the persons who created them and later be reconstructed by others. Such structures can have enormous causal effects without possessing experience of their own.

A law can constrain you without knowing you. A holy book can reorganize a life without possessing a mind. A bureaucracy can preserve procedures without understanding them. An artificial intelligence system can transform and recombine objective knowledge without thereby establishing that it is a knowing subject.

The problem is therefore narrower than a complete theory of cultural transmission, collective coordination, or machine cognition. Two explanatory frames are in conflict. The conventional frame permits societies, institutions, technologies, and fields of knowledge to appear as superordinate epistemic actors.

Abstract it.

The epistemic cycle requires every such appearance to decompose into the same universal process of knowledge creation:

P1→TS→EE→P2P_1 \rightarrow TS \rightarrow EE \rightarrow P_2P1​→TS→EE→P2​

Problem, tentative solution, error elimination, and new problem, instantiated in the minds of individual explainers.

If society requires a special collective process of knowing, this epistemology is not universal and therefore false. Applying the explanation recursively is therefore not an optional extension of the theory. It is a consistency test.

Where do we begin?

1. The solitary standpoint

Knowledge can be objective. Experience cannot.

A scientific paper may exist independently of any present reader. A law may remain in force after everyone who drafted it is dead. A computer program may encode knowledge that no current user could recreate. An institution may preserve procedures across generations.

But every encounter with any of these structures still occurs from one subjective standpoint.

You do not experience society as a whole. You experience particular conversations, rules, media, institutions, sanctions, documents, and other people. From those fragments, you construct an understanding of a social world that is never directly available in its entirety.

That understanding is partial, but partiality is only the first difficulty. What reaches you must also be interpreted. You form conjectures about what others believe, what institutions intend, which norms apply, who has authority, which actions are permitted, what consequences are likely, and what “society” expects.

Those conjectures may be accurate. They may also be wrong.

Nothing unusual has to happen for the error to arise. No class, profession, institution, or historical period has to be especially confused. The possibility follows from the structure of individual experience itself: no person occupies the standpoint of society.

That creates the first explanatory problem. If nobody encounters the social whole directly, how does social life acquire the appearance of unity at all?

Communication seems to offer an answer. One person has an idea, expresses it, and another receives it. Yet that description already assumes the thing that needs explaining: that the same knowledge has somehow crossed from one mind into another.

A speaker has some internal explanatory structure and externalizes part of it through language, gesture, artifact, or action. Another person encounters that expression. The second person does not receive the speaker’s mental state. They reconstruct meaning using their own prior knowledge, language, expectations, current problems, and criticism.

Even successful communication therefore produces overlap rather than identity. Two people can use the same words while possessing different explanatory structures. They can also converge on the same proposition through very different histories.

The important result is not that communication fails. It is that coordination does not require mental identity. Enough overlap can sustain a legal system, laboratory, company, church, school, or family across time. Stable social patterns can emerge from many individual minds reconstructing sufficiently compatible models of one another and of the objective structures through which they act.

The appearance of a social whole begins to dissolve. The next question is whether institutions put it back together at a higher level.

2. Institutions without an institutional mind

Institutions can look as though they escape individual ignorance. A government may contain intelligence agencies, statistical offices, ministries, databases, advisers, courts, and bureaucracies. A corporation may contain finance, legal, engineering, operations, sales, and executive divisions. A university may contain hundreds of disciplines.

The larger the structure, the more knowledge it appears to contain. But the knowledge is distributed. No single mind has access to all of it.

The institution coordinates partial perspectives through reports, forms, meetings, databases, procedures, chains of command, software, policies, and other objective structures. These let people who do not share the same knowledge, and may not even know one another, participate in a stable pattern of action.

That produces a functional whole without producing an epistemic whole.

A government may “know” a statistic in the sense that the statistic exists somewhere in its records. That does not imply that any person making a relevant decision has encountered it, understood it, or interpreted it correctly. A corporation may “decide” to pursue a strategy, but the decision resolves into individual judgments, communications, delegated authority, procedures, and recorded actions.

The institution is causally real. What has not appeared is a new subject of experience.

This distinction matters because objective knowledge can act without awareness. A contract can bind an employee. A diagnostic manual can alter how a clinician interprets a patient. Software can block access to a service. An algorithm can route information. An LLM can produce a candidate explanation. None of these effects requires the artifact itself to possess subjective experience.

The distinction avoids two opposite mistakes. If only persons experience, it does not follow that objective structures are causally unimportant. If objective structures have powerful effects, it does not follow that they are minds.

You are still where the structure becomes lived: encountered, interpreted, criticized, obeyed, resisted, or reconstructed.

That return to you makes coercion less abstract.

A rule can alter another person’s action space through force, threat, punishment, exclusion, loss of resources, legal restriction, surveillance, censorship, or denial of access. An institution can scale the effect, but the causal chain still runs through persons creating, maintaining, or enforcing objective structures that change what other persons can do. A law does not arrest anyone by itself. A school rule does not remove a child from a classroom by itself. A corporate policy does not dismiss an employee by itself.

Constraint can also survive after the enforcing person disappears from the immediate scene. Someone may act within propositions such as: I must obey; people like me should not do this; the expert knows better; this question is inappropriate; this authority is entitled to decide; there is no alternative.

Calling this internalization can make it sound as though “society” has entered you. The more exact sequence is simpler. You encounter socially inherited objective knowledge, reconstruct it, and later use that reconstruction in your own reasoning. The social structure becomes causally effective through your own explanatory system.

External and internal constraint therefore meet at the same point. One changes the available action space. The other can change which alternatives you can see as available at all.

What does this imply about freedom?

3. Freedom, authority, and error correction

Once constraint is described in those terms, freedom acquires a specifically epistemic role.

Error correction requires alternatives. You need some possibility of formulating a problem, considering rival explanations, encountering criticism, testing conjectures through action, and revising conclusions. Coercion can interfere with any of these steps. A censor can restrict rival explanations. A school can forbid a question. A manager can punish dissent. A government can constrain association.

The effect is not exhausted by suffering or domination. Restricting problems, criticisms, associations, or tests can prevent errors from becoming visible and can stabilize false knowledge.

In this inquiry, freedom therefore names more than the absence of restraint. It names a condition in which no other person has unilateral control over the problems you may formulate, the criticisms you may encounter, or the actions through which you may test a conjecture.

That formulation immediately encounters an apparent objection. People do not know equally much about every problem. A patient may rely on a surgeon. A passenger may rely on a pilot. A student may rely on a specialist. Local asymmetries of knowledge are ordinary and often indispensable.

Generalize it.

The difficulty appears when a local asymmetry is transformed into a general rule:

less knowledge→less decision authority\text{less knowledge} \rightarrow \text{less decision authority}less knowledge→less decision authority

The person granted authority remains another partial and fallible mind. A manager may know more about one organizational problem and less about another. An expert may know more about a technical domain and little about the other person’s own goals. A government department may possess aggregated data while misunderstanding the local problem of an individual citizen.

Authority can redistribute decision power. It cannot create an omniscient node.

The solitary standpoint therefore survives every institutional level. The relevant question is no longer whether one person sometimes knows more than another. It is what happens when partial knowledge is converted into durable control over another person’s problem situation.

Can we make this more specific?

Psychology provides a compact case because the whole social structure can return to one person through a single professional encounter.

4. Who knows you?

Psychology is a field, but more tangibly a vast network of researchers, clinicians, reviewers, editors, students, research participants, journals, databases, statistical conventions, professional bodies, diagnostic systems, and institutional rules. Each participant knows only part of the field. What appears as disciplinary knowledge is distributed across individual minds and objective knowledge.

And knowledge moves.

A theory may originate in one person, be revised by another, encoded in a questionnaire, standardized in a manual, taught in a textbook, implemented in software, and applied by a clinician. At each transition, somebody reconstructs what was inherited or transferred.

This explains how a discipline can display more continuity than any individual mind within it. Knowledge persists because it has been externalized. Without that persistence, each generation would have to rediscover everything.

But persistence is neutral about truth. The same machinery can preserve an error. A questionnaire may survive because it is standardized. A construct may persist because journals recognize it. A diagnostic category may remain because reimbursement systems require it. A statistical method may become conventional because software implements it.

Persistence therefore does not imply continued explanatory adequacy. A field can preserve an inherited problem formulation after the reasons for adopting it have become obscure.

Now return to you.

A patient does not encounter “psychology.” The patient encounters one clinician, one questionnaire, one report, one diagnosis, one treatment protocol. The clinician carries compressed objective knowledge inherited from a much larger disciplinary network, but the macro structure becomes locally effective in a relation between particular persons.

The diagnosis can then return to the patient as new objective knowledge. The patient may reconstruct it and begin to interpret themselves through it. A theory used to describe a person can therefore become part of the phenomenon it later measures.

That is the reflexive power of social knowledge.

The claim is not that a field should stop classifying, measuring, or preserving knowledge. The narrower point is explanatory. A disciplinary representation can contain information about a person without containing the whole explanatory history of the person who encounters and reconstructs it.

The same distributed structure also explains how claims can expand while moving through a network.

A researcher reports that a measure correlates with outcome XXX. Another reports a genetic association. A clinician learns that a construct helps classify patients. Later, a journalist says that characteristic XXX is partly genetic, and a parent concludes that a child is naturally like this.

No single person has to traverse the whole inferential chain. Successive local reconstructions can transform the claim until the public or professional conclusion substantially exceeds the original evidence, even when each participant regards their own step as reasonable.

Isn't that why we have meta-analyses and replicable studies?

Meta-analysis cannot automatically correct this kind of inheritance. Different studies may use independent samples while sharing the same psychometric ontology, trait definitions, measurement assumptions, and statistical framework. Meta-analysis can reduce random sampling error. It cannot, merely by aggregating samples, criticize the common frame inherited by all of them.

Replication across samples is therefore not the same thing as replication across problem formulations.

A field can repeatedly corroborate a result inside one inherited frame while leaving that frame itself largely untouched. When you later encounter the result, you usually do not encounter this history of construction and criticism. You encounter a compressed representation whose apparent authority may exceed the criticism that produced it.

The pattern becomes still clearer when the representation leaves the discipline and becomes policy.

Abstract the structure.

Individual observations can be aggregated into a social category. The category can become policy knowledge, and policy can be implemented through institutions. The institutional action can then return as a constraint on an individual:

individual observations→aggregate representation→policy knowledge→institutional action→individual constraint.\text{individual observations} \rightarrow \text{aggregate representation} \\ \rightarrow \text{policy knowledge} \rightarrow \text{institutional action} \rightarrow \text{individual constraint}.individual observations→aggregate representation→policy knowledge→institutional action→individual constraint.

A category may begin as description and end as a rule governing a person. A population is judged vulnerable and access is restricted. A group is judged irrational and an authority structure is introduced. A demographic category becomes a risk class.

No social mind is required. Researchers, administrators, policymakers, and professionals act through objective structures, each seeing only part of the chain. Yet the chain can become stable enough to act back upon the individual from whom the original category was abstracted.

What does the aggregate contain when it returns?

It may contain regularities, risks, correlations, administrative categories, or predictive information. It does not thereby contain the person’s entire problem situation, knowledge history, interpretations, criticisms, goals, or capacity to create explanations not represented in the category. The aggregate can describe something about a person without becoming the person’s explanatory standpoint.

Then the medium really is the message after all?

5. Media and control of the next encounter

The same decomposition changes what “media” means. A medium changes the conditions under which you can encounter, reconstruct, criticize, and continue knowledge.

Explain.

Media are technologies through which objective knowledge is stored, transmitted, selected, or generated. Their history can be read as an expansion of the epistemic functions available to individual minds. Print makes knowledge durable and reproducible. Broadcast lets one communication reach many recipients simultaneously. The internet makes a large body of knowledge individually navigable. Social media reduces geographic and temporal barriers between persons. Generative AI makes objective knowledge responsive to your current problem.

Compress the structure.

The sequence is not a progression toward a social mind. It is a change in how individual minds can encounter objective knowledge.

This becomes important when media effects are compressed into a direct causal shorthand:

medium→child.medium \rightarrow child.medium→child.

Television influences the child. Social media polarizes the child. AI teaches or manipulates the child.

Abstract it.

The shorthand removes the epistemic middle. A more exact sequence is:

knowledge created by persons→technological instantiation→routing / selection / generation→child’s individual reconstruction. \text{knowledge created by persons} \rightarrow \text{technological instantiation} \\ \rightarrow \text{routing / selection / generation} \rightarrow \text{child’s individual reconstruction}.knowledge created by persons→technological instantiation→routing / selection / generation→child’s individual reconstruction.

The medium changes what can reach the child, how quickly it can arrive, how it can be selected, and what forms of response are possible. It does not replace the child’s conjectural actions.

The same physical device can therefore mediate activities with very different epistemic structures: passive viewing, conversation, coding, research, argument, art, technical collaboration, tutoring, or problem solving. “Screen time” compresses all of them into one physical exposure variable.

Does this explain our collective anxieties?

Historical anxieties around new media can now be stated more precisely. The specific concern changes: print can corrupt; radio distracts; television makes passive; the internet exposes; social media fragments; AI homogenizes, replaces, deceives, or overpowers. The recurrence does not show that every concern is false. Each technology has real causal properties.

What persists across the transitions is a change in who can control the next epistemic encounter.

Print weakens local oral gatekeeping. Broadcast weakens geographic information boundaries. Search weakens institutional scarcity. Social media weakens the scarcity of rare-interest communities. AI weakens the scarcity of responsive explanation.

The authority problem survives the technological change because the routing of knowledge changes the problems and criticisms you can encounter.

Social media makes one side of this especially visible. The same network relation that can produce polarization under some conditions can also make highly specialized association possible. Rare interests that were previously geographically isolated can become viable communities. What appears as fragmentation at an aggregate level may, from your standpoint, be increased ability to find other people organized around unusually specific problems.

That does not establish that every resulting community is epistemically healthy. It establishes something narrower: an aggregate category such as “polarization” can hide changes in the topology of association experienced by particular persons.

So the medium is not a subject acting directly on a population. It changes the structure of possible encounters. The consequence still occurs when particular people reconstruct what reaches them and carry that reconstruction into the next problem.

This seems to magnify, not diminish, the stakes with AI.

Generative AI changes this structure again, because the objective knowledge no longer merely waits to be retrieved.

6. Interactive objective knowledge

A search engine retrieves from an existing landscape. A generative model can recombine inherited knowledge around a problem that may not yet correspond to a document, course, or community.

You can therefore move through repeated cycles of problem, tentative solution, criticism, and successor problem while using a technological system as a rapid generator of conjectural material. The system can propose explanations, translate concepts, generate counterexamples, compare theories, critique arguments, produce code, expose hidden assumptions, and suggest rival formulations.

Yes, we're doing it now.

The important change is not that an artificial social mind has appeared. It is that objective knowledge has become interactive.

That interactivity makes the old distinction harder to keep in view. A book contains explanations but does not answer back. A calculator computes but does not converse. An LLM can produce explanations, arguments, code, criticism, plans, stories, and novel combinations. Ordinary language therefore slides easily into claims that AI knows, believes, understands, wants, or is smarter than humans.

Some of that may be harmless shorthand. The ontology remains unsettled.

Structure the problem so we can criticize it more clearly.

Three claims have to stay separate:

  1. AI instantiates objective knowledge.
  2. AI dynamically transforms objective knowledge.
  3. AI is a knowing subject.

The first two can be true without the third having been explained.

The distinction becomes concrete during use. A system can greatly expand the candidate space of explanations without settling which problem matters, which criticism is decisive, or which successor problem should be pursued. You can reject the system’s proposed trajectory and substitute another problem.

Current human-AI knowledge creation is therefore deeply coupled to individual problem selection. This does not prove that AI can never become an autonomous explainer. It identifies a difficult problem of problems within the present explanatory boundary.

The boundary is deeper than anthropomorphism. We still do not possess an explanation of how people create genuinely new explanatory knowledge. We can describe conjecture, criticism, problem selection, error elimination, recombination, analogy, and search. The mechanism by which you produce a genuinely new explanatory idea remains incompletely understood.

That incompleteness changes what can be claimed about machines. If explanatory knowledge creation itself is not yet explained, how can we assert that it exists in machines?

We can identify large stores of objective knowledge, linguistic and statistical structure, search, recombination, planning, problem-responsive generation, and criticism-like procedures. Whether those operations comprise the same universal epistemic process found in persons remains open.

Powerful output in adjacent areas therefore does not establish artificial explanatory knowledge. The limitation is not that AI is weak. The limitation is that the theory of knowledge creation is incomplete. Given the theory, the implementation would follow quickly thereafter. And once explained, the epistemology we're applying here would be revealed as false.

“Superhuman” performance does not remove the gap. A telescope sees farther than a person. A calculator computes faster. A database stores more. A search engine retrieves across scales impossible for one mind. Generative AI extends this familiar pattern into tasks that were previously treated as close proxies for cognition.

A brief digression may be warranted. The superhuman capabilities of AI presently envelope fields that have been conventionally associated with super intelligence. A mathematician may look with despair on the progress of AI and conclude the singularity has arrived. It is still incumbent upon them to explain why. It is insufficient to point to the observations of mathematical proofs and induce from that proof of superhuman creativity. That would merely replicate the problem we've been discussing here. This problem of induction may inform the conjecture that AGI has arrived, we may conjecture AGI in the inductive direction, but it doesn't explain it.

A system can outperform an individual at a task without that performance, by itself, explaining a superior explanatory person. The artifact may do something astonishing. That performance still does not settle what kind of epistemic entity produced it, or how

Technology also makes the earlier political structure easier to scale. A censor once had to act repeatedly; a technical system can encode the censorship rule. A teacher once had to control access manually; a platform can automate restriction. A government once had to distribute instructions through officials; software can implement them instantly.

The same machinery that expands access to explanation can therefore expand constraint. Technology amplifies objective knowledge, including both explanatory and coercive knowledge. The relevant question is which knowledge has been encoded, by whom, and how that encoding changes the problem and action spaces available to particular persons.

The same technology can widen one person’s field of possible criticism while narrowing another’s. It can weaken one gatekeeper and create another. Once again, the explanatory unit is not “technology” acting as a subject. It is the relation between individual minds and the objective structures through which their possible encounters and actions are changed.

7. The recursive test

The argument can now be turned back on itself.

If the epistemology is universal, every apparent social mind must decompose to individuals. Psychology resolves into psychologists and objective disciplinary knowledge. A government resolves into officials, citizens, laws, records, technologies, and communications. A religion resolves into persons, texts, institutions, rituals, and inherited explanations. A corporation resolves into employees, managers, procedures, contracts, software, and capital structures. Science resolves into scientists, instruments, papers, datasets, methods, and criticism.

AI must also decompose into technology, objective knowledge, training processes, system design, and interactions with individual people unless and until an independent explanation establishes a new epistemic subject.

If any one of these cases requires a special collective mind, then this universal account has failed. That is what makes recursion a test rather than an illustration.

The conventional frame can say that society believes, science knows, institutions decide, or AI understands because aggregate descriptions are useful. The epistemic frame keeps asking what the shorthand has compressed: Which individuals know what? Which objective structures preserve what? How is that knowledge communicated? Where is it reconstructed? Which person acts? Which structure changes the action space?

Some might call this intellectual dishonesty, or moving the goalposts, or denial of reality. We would say, reality is common sense, but epistemology is not, and universality provides the grounds.

Collective phenomena remain. What disappears is the need to add a collective subject merely because the pattern is stable or powerful. Society can be causally real, institutionally durable, and epistemically powerful without becoming a mind above the persons who compose and encounter it.

The epistemic frame can be compressed to three elements: individual knowers, objective knowledge, and fallible coordination between them. It's not a wisdom of the crowds, because crowds can't possess wisdom. It is a wisdom between the crowds, in the objective knowledge and interfaces through which individual minds exchange, preserve, criticize, route, and reconstruct knowledge.

Every social structure in this account must be explainable in those terms. If one cannot be, the universality of epistemology has failed.

But survival produces a harder question. If society has no mind of its own, how does knowledge created by particular people become socially persistent? How is it reconstructed by people who did not create it, preserved across generations, transformed as it moves, and stabilized strongly enough to shape the problems of persons not yet born?

And if freedom or coercion changes which reconstructions remain possible, how does that alter the evolution of socially persistent knowledge?
