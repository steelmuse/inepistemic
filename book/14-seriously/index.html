The Unfinished Explainer

XIV. Taking Explanations Personally

Related material for this chapter

  • Visual companionInfographic
  • ReferencesEndnotes

Inside your epistemic mind, we locate imagery, memory, inner speech, salience, skill, intuition, and other forms of inexplicit knowledge. What becomes explicit is only a narrow surface over an immense and largely inaccessible universe.

Making knowledge explicit gives your creative criticism something to chew on. But the explanation may still remain isolated from everything else you know. What happens when you allow its consequences to travel? What does it mean for you to possess an explanation, to really take an explanation personally?

Possession cannot mean merely repeating an explanation, assenting to it, defending it, or placing it beside other beliefs you also happen to hold. An explanation can have consequences outside the problem that first made it useful. It can connect to other explanations, rule out possibilities, expose contradictions, and create new problems. Call that capacity reach.

The difficulty is that almost everything in ordinary intellectual life can interrupt it. Knowledge is divided into fields. Professional jurisdictions are constructed. One question belongs to psychology, another to economics, another to physics, another to philosophy. Expertise can then acquire a second role beyond supplying specialized knowledge: it can become a way of deciding where criticism is allowed to cross a boundary.

You can reproduce the same problematic structure inside yourself. An explanation can work in one part of your life while its implications are held away from another. Contradictions can remain dormant for years. An identity can remain protected from an idea you would readily apply elsewhere.

Until something stops fitting. A new fact makes an old belief uncomfortable. A moral judgment collides with something you know about another person. A theory you admire produces a consequence you do not want. The contradiction may still be inexplicit, but the world has become harder to make sense of. That experience may be one of the places where epistemology becomes embodied in the epistemic mind.

To take an explanation personally is to allow it to create problems for everything else you think you know.

The claim is first epistemic. Its moral consequences arrive later, if you choose them.

1. One Reality Gives Explanations Reach

Under one reality, explanations that describe it may begin at different levels and solve different problems, but they cannot finally require incompatible reality. A theory of mind cannot require something physics excludes. A theory of knowledge cannot require a transformation that computation makes impossible. A psychological explanation cannot define people in a way that makes their known capacity for explanatory creativity impossible.

Explanations do not have to reduce to one another. They do not need the same vocabulary or the same object. They only have to remain mutually possible. That mutual possibility may be called consilience.

Now another consequence becomes visible. If an explanation describes structure in the one world, its consequences do not stop at the disciplinary border around the problem that produced it. They can travel. Evolution reaches beyond one species. Computation reaches beyond one computer. A theory of knowledge that claims universality reaches beyond philosophy and science into childhood, invention, institutions, morality, engineering, and ordinary life.

A problem in personality genetics generated a criticism of the transport from populations to persons. That exposed a social problem, then a problem about collective minds, then problems about freedom, abstraction, criticism, coordination, morality, justification, and inexplicit knowledge. The subject changed because the explanation kept producing consequences, and those consequences kept producing new problems. That is reach in practice; explanations traveling.

It also sharpens what it means to possess an explanation. You can say that people are fallible while treating one authority as incapable of serious error. You can say that individuals are free while making categorical exceptions when freedom becomes inconvenient. You can say there is one reality while protecting incompatible explanations inside separate fields.

In each case the explanation is still professed. What has been blocked is its ability to alter the surrounding problem situation.

Compare it:

professing an explanation≠taking an explanation seriously.\text{professing an explanation} \neq \text{taking an explanation seriously}.professing an explanation=taking an explanation seriously.

Taking an explanation seriously means allowing what it explains to change what can remain unproblematic elsewhere.

2. Domains Are Not Jurisdictions

Specialization creates real differences in knowledge. A physicist may know things a psychologist does not. A surgeon may know things a patient does not. A statistician may see a modeling error everyone else misses. No individual can reconstruct all objective knowledge, so fields, technical language, professional communities, and specialized methods solve genuine coordination problems.

The problem begins when a difference in knowledge is turned into a protected boundary around criticism:

specialized knowledge→exclusive epistemic authority.\text{specialized knowledge} \rightarrow \text{exclusive epistemic authority}.specialized knowledge→exclusive epistemic authority.

Then a physicist is told not to criticize a psychologist, an engineer is told that a philosophical assumption in the system lies outside engineering, or an outsider's contradiction is dismissed because the outsider lacks the recognized credential.

The local knowledge may still matter. Expertise in cardiology does not supply the knowledge needed for aircraft maintenance. That is a difference in knowledge, not a difference in the kind of person allowed to expose a contradiction. Contradictions do not acquire professional memberships.

An explanation can therefore have a bounded domain of application without having a protected jurisdiction against criticism. The phrase “stay in your lane” can conceal the opposite idea: knowledge belongs to the people authorized to possess it. Under that picture, the expert possesses technical truth, the discipline possesses its object, and everyone else receives conclusions.

An inductive account of knowledge makes the move especially tempting. If knowledge is accumulated from observations, then the person with more observations appears to possess more of the knowledge itself. The outsider has seen less, so the outsider can be classified as epistemically weaker before the criticism has even been reconstructed.

But once an explanation exists in a form that can be reconstructed independently of its original knower, biography no longer fixes its reach. A mathematical proof can outlive the mathematician who first understood it. A scientific theory can be reconstructed centuries later. A line of code can embody an explanation its current maintainer understands only partly.

This is one of the consequences of objective knowledge: knowledge can become available for reconstruction without remaining inside the person who created it. That does not make reconstruction automatic. It does change the question. The social identity of the critic cannot settle whether the criticism succeeds.

The relevant question is:

What problem does the criticism expose?

3. When Reach Becomes Personal

The external silo has an internal analogue.

Picture your mind as a collection of beliefs: scientific beliefs, moral beliefs, beliefs about yourself, beliefs about other people, professional knowledge, family knowledge, private intuitions. That picture is much too simple. Much of your epistemic mind is inexplicit. It appears as expectation, salience, memory, emotional orientation, embodied skill, perceptual organization, habitual distinction, and tacit causal structure.

You do not merely hold propositions. You inhabit an epistemic mind. Your mind contains contradictions, but it cannot literally become several realities. It manifests your reality.

Compare it.

Let RRR represent reality and MiM_iMi​ the epistemic mind of person iii. Then:

Mi≠R.M_i \neq R.Mi​=R.

Your epistemic mind is not reality itself. You do not possess complete, unmediated access to what exists. But MiM_iMi​ is not arbitrary either. You act in reality. Expectations succeed or fail. Objects resist. Other people surprise you. Predictions break. Tools work or do not.

There is therefore some continuing, fallible structural correspondence:

Mi∼R.M_i \sim R.Mi​∼R.

The relation is not identity and not perfect representation. It is correspondence good enough, in some respects, for you to navigate the world and revise your understanding of it. This is the conjectured isomorphism.

Now the internal problem becomes clearer. You do not ordinarily experience MiM_iMi​ as a model displayed beside a separate reality. You walk into a room and experience the room. You see a face rather than consciously receiving uninterpreted wavelengths and then constructing one. When inexplicit knowledge about another person predicts danger, what appears in experience may simply be unease. Your epistemic mind is the medium through which reality becomes intelligible to you.

That is why a contradiction can first appear as a disturbance in intelligibility. A trusted explanation produces a consequence you cannot absorb. Someone you classified one way acts in a way the classification cannot explain. A principle you apply confidently in one setting becomes painful when it reaches your own child. A theory of yourself can no longer account for what you have done. The contradiction may not yet be explicit. Something simply feels wrong.

Criticism sometimes begins in felt disequilibrium. Inexplicit knowledge becoming available for explicit thought when a problem makes it relevant. Put those together and a further conjecture appears:

the pressure toward explanatory integration may be one way epistemology is instantiated in you.

You may take knowledge seriously because, in some sense, you already live inside it. This is not a call for reductionism. You can love mathematics and music, be playful at home and procedural at work, and possess different forms of knowledge for different problems. Different identities, roles, habits, and forms of attention can coexist without difficulty. Plurality is not contradiction.

The problem appears only when two explanations make incompatible claims about the same reality. Then the incompatibility remains a problem even if it stays dormant or you would prefer not to integrate it. Multiplicity does not create multiple realities.

A stronger escape would be to declare some parts of personhood permanently outside explanation: the soul, love, art, intuition, intelligence, identity, mystery. The distinction here is not between explaining everything now and explaining nothing. It is between saying that no explanation is yet available and saying that no explanation is possible in principle. The first statement leaves a problem open. The second makes a claim about the permanent limits of knowledge.

That claim is itself explanatory enough to be criticized.

4. Expertise Supplies Criticism, Not Sovereignty

The Dunning–Kruger effect provides a small case in which these ideas converge. Its popular compression is that incompetent people do not know enough to recognize their own incompetence. There is a plausible mechanism inside that slogan: if you lack knowledge, you may also lack the distinctions needed to notice some of your errors. Learning can reveal errors that were previously unavailable to you as problems.

The trouble begins when that mechanism is compressed again.

Abstract it:

knows less→cannot evaluate→does not know that they cannot evaluate→their judgment has little epistemic standing.\text{knows less} \rightarrow \text{cannot evaluate} \rightarrow \text{does not know that they cannot evaluate} \rightarrow \text{their judgment has little epistemic standing}.knows less→cannot evaluate→does not know that they cannot evaluate→their judgment has little epistemic standing.

The framework here offers a different description. Suppose that at time ttt you possess knowledge KtK_tKt​. That knowledge makes some errors visible and others invisible. It also supplies criteria CtC_tCt​ for evaluating performance:

Kt→Ct→self-modelt.K_t \rightarrow C_t \rightarrow \text{self-model}_t.Kt​→Ct​→self-modelt​.

Then you learn. New distinctions become available. The criteria change. Errors that previously did not exist for you as explicit problems become visible:

Kt+1→Ct+1→new self-model.K_{t+1} \rightarrow C_{t+1} \rightarrow \text{new self-model}.Kt+1​→Ct+1​→new self-model.

No defective class of knower is required. Your current knowledge limits the criticisms currently available to you. Expertise matters because an expert may possess criticisms you cannot yet formulate. If an engineer identifies a structural dependency in a bridge design that you do not understand, your ignorance is real.

But the difference is:

knowledge possessed,\text{knowledge possessed},knowledge possessed,

not:

kind of knower.\text{kind of knower}.kind of knower.

You can learn. The expert can be wrong. An outsider can expose a contradiction. Unequal knowledge can explain unequal access to criticism without creating an exclusive jurisdiction over truth.

That makes other people epistemically important for a different reason. If your epistemic mind is partial with respect to a subject (the universal state of ignorance), another person may carry an observation you lack, an explanation you have never encountered, or a problem your abstractions do not make visible. They may carry precisely the perturbation your epistemic mind cannot generate from inside itself. But the perturbation is useful only if you recover what it means.

Suppose another person says “AI is unsafe,” “experts know better,” or “children need discipline.” Each sentence is compressed. It may carry observations, causal claims, moral judgments, inherited ideas, personal history, vague unease, or an explanatory criticism. The surface sentence does not tell you which.

The first operation is therefore to open the compression and conjecture the structure that produced it. The other person can then correct that reconstruction before criticism begins.

Call this operation decompression:

expression→decompression→conjectured meaning→correction→criticism.\text{expression} \rightarrow \text{decompression} \rightarrow \text{conjectured meaning} \rightarrow \text{correction} \rightarrow \text{criticism}.expression→decompression→conjectured meaning→correction→criticism.

This differs from steel-manning. If the other person produces explanation EAE_AEA​ and you replace it with a stronger explanation EBE_BEB​, you may have improved the debate while losing the thing they actually meant. Decompression does not improve the other person's explanation. It attempts to recover it. Steel-manning often does the opposite: it strengthens the compression, making it more resilient to criticism.

Communication can't give you direct access to another mind. Recipients reconstruct meaning; two reconstructions can be produced through radically different internal machinery. Taking another person's explanation personally therefore cannot mean accepting it. It means reconstructing enough of its content that the explanation can actually be criticized.

Once that happens, an objection changes status. It is no longer merely an obstacle to your explanation. It may contain the missing problem. The other person may still be confused or wrong. The point is that social classification no longer decides the matter in advance. That is the form of criticism needed here: criticism creative enough to let another epistemic mind change your problem.

5. Many Tools, One Epistemological Problem

A more serious objection now appears. Perhaps there is no single epistemology at all. Perhaps there are only different ways of knowing: induction, Bayesian inference, falsification, deduction, abduction, intuition, testimony, decision theory, statistics, common sense. Different problems call for different tools, and rationality consists in choosing the right one.

That picture is attractive because it looks tolerant and practical. It also resembles ordinary scientific and technical work. Researchers use statistics. Engineers use models. Doctors reason from evidence. People update beliefs. And of course, a Popperian conjecture is guesswork.

But the existence of many epistemic operations does not yet establish many epistemologies. Bayesian inference has broad applications. Statistical estimation is commonplace. Deduction is indispensable. Models and observations matter. Heuristics can generate candidate solutions. Intuition can be the first explicit surface of an inexplicit disturbance.

The objects are different. An explanation asserts structure. A model represents some of that structure for a problem. An experiment creates a critical situation. An observation occurs within that situation. Probability distributes weight across a represented possibility space. A heuristic proposes a move. None of those descriptions makes the operations unreal or unimportant.

They can still participate in one broader process:

P→TS→EE→P2.P \rightarrow TS \rightarrow EE \rightarrow P_2.P→TS→EE→P2​.

The pluralism may therefore be functional rather than foundational.

Bayesian reasoning makes the distinction concrete. Suppose a gambler knows the rules of a game, the deck is specified, the outcomes are represented, and the relevant probabilities can be calculated. Probabilistic reasoning may be exactly the right tool. The present epistemology has no reason to deny that.

The problem begins only when the tool is asked to explain the creation of the represented space in which it operates. Probability can redistribute weight among available possibilities. But a creative problem may consist in discovering that the variables are wrong, that the relevant hypothesis does not yet exist, that the evaluative criterion needs to change, or that the problem itself has been mis-stated. Probability can operate brilliantly once those structures exist. That does not yet explain where they came from.

The same difficulty appears if rationality is defined as choosing the right tool. Use Bayes when Bayes applies. Use deduction when deduction applies. Use intuition when intuition is useful. The instruction merely moves the explanatory burden into the word right. What makes one operation appropriate when the problem itself can change?

What unifies the cases here is not a common surface procedure. It is a direction. A musician can notice an error inexplicitly. A physicist can derive a contradiction. A statistician can expose a model failure. Different operations can all alter the problem by exposing error.

That direction is what this epistemology calls rationality:

Rationality is an orientation toward exposing and eliminating error.

The visible tools can differ while the epistemological problem remains the same.

6. Universality Means Vulnerability

The epistemology explored here does not present as one useful perspective among others. It claims that knowledge grows evolutionarily, through problems, conjecture, criticism, error elimination, and successor problems. That claim is universal. So it is vulnerable wherever knowledge grows by an incompatible mechanism.

Epistemic pluralism therefore cannot be dismissed as a matter of taste. It is a direct criticism of the universality claim. The anti-inductivist part is especially exposed. If explanatory knowledge can actually be logically derived from repeated observations, or more generally, if knowledge may be derived from data, then a central part of this epistemology is not merely incomplete. It is false.

Abstract it.

The break condition can be stated directly. If there exists a valid operation:

O1,O2,…,On→EO_1, O_2, \ldots, O_n \rightarrow EO1​,O2​,…,On​→E

in which observations logically generate the explanatory content without a creative conjecture, then the account of knowledge creation offered here has failed. Redefining that operation after the fact so that it fits the theory would only protect the theory from the consequence of its own universality.

Metaphysics does not remove this exposure. Epistemology concerns the structure of knowledge and its relation to reality, so an isolated observation cannot stand outside all explanation and interpret itself. A measurement already sits inside a model, an experiment, a theory, and a problem situation. But that does not make epistemology immune from criticism. A rival explanation with greater reach can expose a contradiction here. Engineering can reveal a knowledge-creating mechanism this account excludes. A supposedly impossible operation can become a new problem for the theory.

The relevant standard is not whether one observation can mechanically refute metaphysics. It is whether the explanation survives contact with everything else we know.

Beliefs and credences occupy the same position. A model can begin with beliefs, update credences when evidence arrives, and derive actions. That may solve real problems. But the present explanation of knowledge growth does not require every epistemic change to pass through an explicitly represented degree of belief. You can act on a tentative solution without calculating a credence. You can discover a problem that was not represented, change an evaluative criterion, or construct an explanation that did not exist among the prior options.

Conventional concepts of belief or decision-making do not acquire foundational status merely because they are conventional.

The openness runs both ways. If a future theory of belief or decision acquires universal explanatory reach and collides with this account, the collision becomes part of the problem situation. No explanation is immune. Including this one.

7. Engineering Must Reach Both Ways

Engineering now becomes the surface for a severe test because epistemology has crossed into a domain where explanations can be embodied in artifacts.

If knowledge creation requires open problems, conjectural variation, criticism, revision, and the possibility of changing evaluative criteria, then an architecture intended to create knowledge provides a place where those claims can acquire technical consequences. If the explanation has no consequences at all for such an architecture, its supposed reach has stopped exactly where the test becomes concrete.

But the direction cannot run only from philosophy into engineering. Engineering creates new objective knowledge. Systems fail in unanticipated ways. Architectures surprise their designers. Capabilities appear that were not predicted. Mechanisms that seemed necessary can turn out to be incidental. If an engineered system creates knowledge through a process incompatible with this epistemology, then the engineering has created a criticism of the epistemology.

Abstract it.

The relation is reciprocal:

epistemology→engineering constraints\text{epistemology} \rightarrow \text{engineering constraints}epistemology→engineering constraints

and:

engineering→criticism of epistemology.\text{engineering} \rightarrow \text{criticism of epistemology}.engineering→criticism of epistemology.

Neither side owns the other.

The conversation that produced this work adds a recursive case. The artificial system participating in the work has been deliberately configured, or harnessed, around the epistemology under discussion. It represents inquiry through problems, tentative solutions, error elimination, and successor problems. It is constrained against treating authority, confidence, consensus, or supporting evidence as epistemic foundations. It is asked to expose contradictions rather than merely defend outputs. It also distinguishes the human operator and originator of the problem situation from the machine's role in retrieval, recombination, formalization, and execution.

That arrangement proves nothing about the epistemology. A system can be built according to a false explanation. The significance is different. If an explanation claims consequences for invention, one way to expose those consequences to criticism is to instantiate them and see what breaks. This system is one such attempt.

Its characteristic failures would therefore matter. A formal cycle of problems and error elimination might suppress useful creativity. The system might fail to originate problems without human intervention. The distinction between conjecture and induction might become impossible to maintain in implementation. Enforced criticism might collapse into ritualized self-critique. An architecture designed for explanatory openness might become procedurally rigid.

Those failures would be more than defects in a tool. They would be observations created by embodiment: criticisms that were unavailable while the epistemology remained purely philosophical. That is what reciprocal reach looks like. The explanation constrains the artifact; the artifact gets a chance to break the explanation. Reality kicks back.

8. Abduction as a Test of Creative Knowledge

One proposal at the frontier of artificial intelligence sharpens the engineering test. Current systems may be extraordinarily capable statistical and inductive machines, yet still lack abduction: inference to the best explanation. Perhaps that missing operation is the bridge to genuine explanatory creativity.

Abstract it.

The proposal has a familiar form:

observations→candidate explanations→best explanation.\text{observations} \rightarrow \text{candidate explanations} \rightarrow \text{best explanation}.observations→candidate explanations→best explanation.

If that process genuinely explains open-ended explanatory creativity, then the epistemology explored here has a serious problem.

The pressure appears inside the phrase best explanation. Best according to what? Reach, simplicity, likelihood, fit, prior probability, utility, or some learned objective? Whatever the answer, the architecture requires criteria by which alternatives can be compared.

But there is a much harder problem. A creative solution may not merely satisfy the criteria available before the solution. It may alter what the problem is and what counts as a good answer.

The structure can therefore change from one problem-and-criteria pair to another:

(P1,C1)t→TS→(P2,C2)t+1,(P_1,C_1)_t \rightarrow TS \rightarrow (P_2,C_2)_{t+1},(P1​,C1​)t​→TS→(P2​,C2​)t+1​,

where CiC_iCi​ represents evaluative criteria. The map changes with the destination.

That temporal asymmetry matters. After a creative leap, the successful explanation reorganizes the observations, exposes weaknesses in earlier theories, and may look like the obvious best explanation. Retrospectively, the history can be compressed into a clean sequence: the thinker observed the evidence and inferred the best explanation.

But the later description may erase the event that needs explaining. Before the explanation existed, the relevant hypothesis was not necessarily one member of an already represented set. Before the problem was reformulated, the criterion by which the later answer looks best may not have existed either.

Calling the process abduction can therefore restate the finished achievement without yet explaining the origin of the new hypothesis, problem, or evaluative criteria.

That criticism does not imply that abductive methods are a dead end. Quite the contrary. They may improve hypothesis ranking, organize observations into more useful candidate explanations, expose latent relationships, strengthen scientific tools, and generate outputs that increasingly resemble explanatory reasoning.

Current induction engines may become better induction engines.

The conjecture here is narrower:

abduction, if it remains inference within inherited or learned problem and evaluation structures, will not by itself produce the open-ended explanatory creativity attributed here to people.

And further, where creative variation in this strong sense is introduced, we will trace the origins to people. That conjecture has to remain breakable. Otherwise the conjecture would define away every contrary result.

The framework attributes several capacities to people: originating new problems, generating explanations not logically derived from prior observations, creatively criticizing those explanations, transforming evaluative criteria, and creating successor problem situations as novel structures. If an abductive architecture produces those capacities, then the epistemology has a serious problem. It doesn't yet have a rival explanation, but a serious problem.

This posture sits comfortably, if not obviously, with the knowledge that inductive inference does not exist (in the logical sense), and the much older observation that people conjecture in the inductive direction, and the engineered reality of systems that process data inductively (in a mechanical sense as engineers describe it). This may be confusing, but it is not confused. We don't argue about words, we decompress them.

AI therefore occupies an unusual position. These systems can produce surprising outputs, recombine enormous bodies of objective knowledge, surface analogies, generate candidate explanations, help expose contradictions across fields, simulate criticism, and preserve explanatory surfaces larger than you can hold in working memory.

Those capacities still leave the personhood problem open. Remaining questions may concern the active problem for the machine and where that problem came from. They may also concern what makes one criticism matter rather than another, and whether the system can transform the criteria by which its own explanations are evaluated. And if so, how other universal explainers may recognize the latent evaluative criteria. The questions are open because the engineering has not yet dissolved these distinctions.

At this point the explanation turns on itself, and you. Taking explanations personally applies to psychology, physics, engineering, probability, AI, other people, your own internal knowledge, and the universal epistemology that connected them. Universality does not create a privileged final layer. It increases the surface for further criticism.

9. Moral Knowledge Enters the Same World

The moral consequence arrives only after that epistemic structure is in place. Moral explanations concern problems involving persons, suffering, freedom, responsibility, coercion, flourishing, and action. If they are explanations, they inhabit the same reality as every other explanation. Their consequences can therefore collide with what we know elsewhere, and criticism can expose those collisions.

That is the sense in which moral seriousness is explanatory seriousness. The claim does not make an authority true because the subject is morally important, and it does not insulate a moral explanation from criticism. It places moral knowledge inside the same consilient reality.

The familiar is–ought problem immediately creates pressure. An explanation of reality does not entail a moral obligation. So what kind of action-consequence can an explanation generate without smuggling an authority into the premises?

Return to the gambler. Suppose the active problem is to maximize expected return under specified rules, and the probabilities are known. The calculation can identify a current solution to that stated problem. You remain free to choose otherwise.

A contradiction appears only if all of the explanatory structure is held fixed while the opposite action is also described as the better solution to the same problem. If the problem changes, the action can change. If the probabilities are rejected, the calculation changes. If another value enters, the problem was not the one first stated. If some hidden condition matters, the explanatory structure was incomplete.

Something has to move. That distinction gives a precise role to the phrase explanatory ought. A coercive ought derives force from an authority: you must do this because the authority requires it. An explanatory ought does something different: given this problem and this explanation, this action currently functions as a tentative solution.

The second statement does not entail obedience. It exposes a consequence inside a problem situation. And people may disobey epistemically by altering that problem situation. You can reject the problem, criticize the explanation, introduce another value, construct a different solution, act inconsistently, or accept the consequences. Freedom remains because explanatory structure does not remove your capacity to create a new knowledge.

Earlier claims became morally relevant without being converted into oughts. If people are universal explainers, institutions that classify some people as epistemically inferior assume an explanatory problem. If criticism is required for error correction, structures that prohibit criticism acquire another. If objective knowledge can coordinate heterogeneous people without merging their minds, claims that deep conformity is necessary for social order become criticizable. If children possess less knowledge without possessing a lesser kind of mind, authority relations built on the opposite assumption collide with a theory of personhood. If coercion closes problem situations, new coordination technologies can alter the conditions under which older coercive arrangements once semmed necessary.

Epistemology does not deduce a moral code from facts. It causes some moral explanations to become inconsilient with existing explanations and authorities.

Moral progress can then be described as a change in explanatory integration. A practice may once look necessary or harmless. A better explanation of personhood, a newly visible form of suffering, or a technology that removes an old coordination constraint can make the old explanation harder to sustain. Criticism from people subjected to the practice can expose a problem the earlier description suppressed. The world of moral knowledge reorganizes because its internal explanatory structure changed.

That creates one more danger. “Take explanations seriously” can itself be turned into an authority: the science is settled, therefore obey, or this theory has consequences, therefore compliance follows. That reverses the argument. Explanatory seriousness increases exposure to criticism; it does not convert an explanation into a command.

The strongest consequence of taking an explanation seriously may still be the criticism that destroys it.

10. Consilience Becomes Personal

The same structure changes what it means to take another person seriously. Their visible exterior must be only a compression of a vast epistemic mind. Taking the person seriously therefore does not mean assuming they are right or suspending criticism. It means reconstructing the problem, observation, inherited idea, causal structure, or unease that the expression is carrying far enough for criticism to reach the thing they actually intended.

That is the interpersonal form of decompression developed earlier. The person can reject your reconstruction. Only then does the remaining disagreement become a useful object of criticism. The point matters because the epistemic mind inside you was never purely private in the first place.

Imagine living almost entirely alone while possessing a modern scientific worldview. Your internal knowledge would still contain language, mathematics, physical explanations, inherited moral concepts, technologies, and theories created by people you never met. Isolation would not create a different reality. It would leave you densely connected to objective knowledge produced by others.

Even solitary thought is socially and historically populated. Language carries distinctions inherited from other minds. A scientific worldview carries centuries of criticism. A programming language embodies explanations about computation and abstraction. You reconstruct these structures locally, but they remain part of your epistemic mind. The relation between MiM_iMi​ and RRR is therefore mediated not only by your direct encounters with physical reality but by enormous bodies of objective knowledge.

You carry fragments of civilization inside your epistemic mind. The external and internal arguments now meet. Explanatory enclosure is artificial not only because contradictions cross disciplinary boundaries, but because the knowledge inside you already crosses them. The lanes were crossed long before anyone told you to stay inside them.

This coming together of knowledge is called consilience. Here the same idea becomes personal. Consilience is not only the condition in which physics and biology remain mutually possible. It is also the provisional intelligibility of your own experienced world. Your scientific explanation does not destroy your theory of personhood. Your moral explanation can coexist with what you know happened. Your memory and your account of yourself can still occupy one reality.

For a time, the system is in explanatory equilibrium. Then criticism arrives. Something stops fitting. The equilibrium breaks. A problem becomes visible. Knowledge changes. Taking explanations personally is not an extra rule added beside consilience. It is the process by which consilience remains open rather than hardening into isolation.

11. Maximum Exposure

The growing coherence produces a danger of its own. Genetics, psychology, society, freedom, abstraction, criticism, coordination, morality, justification, inexplicit knowledge, and now explanatory consilience have been forced together. As the connections accumulate, the framework can begin to feel inevitable.

That feeling is not a warrant. Internal coherence is not proof. Reach is not justification. Consilience is provisional. A framework with greater reach also has more surfaces on which contradiction can appear. Its success therefore increases the number of places where it can fail.

Some of those break conditions are already visible. If induction can create explanatory knowledge from observations without a creative conjecture, the epistemology is in jeopardy. If probabilistic updating can explain the open-ended creation of new problem situations and evaluative criteria, the epistemology is problematic. If a theory of belief or decision acquires universal reach incompatible with this account, the collision has to be resolved. If engineering creates an artificial person through mechanisms the epistemology excludes, the epistemology is denied. If moral explanations require a fundamentally different account of knowledge from every other domain, the universality claim is false, and so is the epistemology.

These are not concessions added to make epistemology sound cautious. They follow from the claim that explanations acquire consequences outside the place where they began.

Now apply that claim to itself.

Take seriously that you should take explanations seriously.

The sentence becomes meaningful only if it is allowed to create problems for every other explanation you hold. Rival explanations, engineering results, moral consequences, internal contradictions, and the architecture of this project all become possible sources of criticism.

If the recursion exposes a contradiction, the next problem has appeared. That is the epistemic cycle operating, not an exception to it.

Nothing in this requires omniscience or infallibility. Contradictions can remain inexplicit for a long time. Expertise can still supply criticisms you lack. Statistical and probabilistic tools can still solve real problems. Two people can reconstruct each other accurately and still disagree. Explanations can expose consequences without commanding obedience.

The requirement is narrower:

do not protect a good explanation from its consequences.

The movement is now visible, but the mechanism is not. Why does one contradiction become irresistible while another remains compartmentalized? Why does one criticism transform your evaluative criteria while another fails to enter the problem at all? How does an inexplicit mismatch become explicit? How can a physically instantiated epistemic mind reorganize while preserving the continuity of the person who experiences the change?

Those questions are now harder because the proposed answer cannot be merely psychological. The mechanism has to remain compatible with this evolutionary epistemology, with computation, with physical instantiation, and with the personal continuity of you.

If taking explanations personally is one way epistemology is instantiated in people, then we still lack an explanation of how you do it. That successor problem makes explicit the question that has been waiting underneath all along:

What is a person?
