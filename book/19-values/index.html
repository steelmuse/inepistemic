What Moves You

XIX. The Value Bomb

You are not exhausted by your present explanations. A commitment does not specify the future you. New problems can become visible, new criticisms can arrive, and new explanations can be created. You can change what you think matters, what you count as progress, and even what you regard as the problem.

That open future creates a problem: Some explanations may become sufficiently general, important, and compressed that we stop experiencing them as explanations at all. We call them **values.

A value can sound like the place where explanation ends:

I value liberty. I value truth. I value a natural childhood. I value equality.

The grammar is revealing. The value appears as an internal fact about you. You may feel it sincerely; it may organize your action and appear morally serious. Because it is called a value, asking for an explanation can begin to sound almost improper.

Why do you value liberty? Equality? Tradition? Technological abstinence?

At some point the answer may become:

Because I do.

Or:

Because it is right.

Or:

Because those are my values.

The explanatory chain has stopped, but the value has not stopped doing explanatory work. The same unexplained value can now be used to explain why an action is good, why an institution is legitimate, why a technology is dangerous, why a child should be protected, why another person is immoral, why an opposing idea should be excluded, or why criticism itself has gone too far.

That inversion is the problem. A compressed explanation becomes an explanatory premise, and the premise can then be turned back upon its own criticism. The value begins by summarizing an explanation. It ends by protecting itself from explanation.

That structure can scale. It can help make religions and ideologies unusually difficult to criticize. It can turn rival values into pollution, people into viruses, objective knowledge into contamination, and technologies into villains. It can also turn a real liberal problem about tolerance into a blunt permission to exclude others.

The conclusion is not that values are inherently problematic. Some values may be useful precisely because they compress explanations with great reach. But useful compression is not explanatory foundation. If a value is to preserve explanatory knowledge rather than replace it, the road back to the explanation has to remain available.

1. Values Are Compressed Explanations

Consider:

I value liberty.

It appears to name a moral primitive. But epistemically, liberty is not treated that way. We arrived at freedom through explanation.

You are fallible. Knowledge grows through conjecture and criticism. Criticism requires alternatives. Coercion can remove alternatives, punish dissent, suppress communication, restrict experimentation, and narrow the problems you are permitted to pursue.

That gives an explanatory structure:

fallibility+criticism+open alternatives→epistemic importance of freedom.\text{fallibility} + \text{criticism} + \text{open alternatives} \rightarrow \text{epistemic importance of freedom}.fallibility+criticism+open alternatives→epistemic importance of freedom.

Only after constructing that structure do we obtain the compressed expression:

liberty matters.

The explanation comes first. The compressed value comes later.

The same relation appears elsewhere. Truth is not valuable merely because the word truth has moral force. It begins with realism and from the possibility that explanations can be wrong. Error matters only if there is something for the explanation to be wrong about.

Creation is not valuable merely because creation sounds noble. You can create a cruel institution, a better weapon, or a more efficient propaganda system. What matters is the explanatory structure in which creation occurs and the problems it solves or creates.

Equality is not self-validating either. It can acquire explanatory force through deeper claims about persons, coercion, knowledge, authority, and the absence of any demonstrated epistemic caste entitled to rule the rest.

The general sequence is:

P→E→VP \rightarrow E \rightarrow VP→E→V

where PPP is a problem, EEE an epistemic structure, and VVV a compressed value.

The value is shorthand. It is not the origin of the explanation.

The error appears when the sequence is reversed:

V→E.V \rightarrow E.V→E.

Now liberty asserts why something is good. Equality asserts why something is good. Tradition or nature does the same work. The compression is being used as though it were the structure that originally gave the compression meaning.

Compression itself is not a defect. You could not think or coordinate without it. A scientific term can compress an enormous body of explanation. A mathematical symbol can preserve relations that would otherwise require pages to restate. A technical interface can hide complexity while preserving access to the function built upon it.

Values can work similarly. When you say:

I value truth,

that phrase may carry realism, fallibility, criticism, scientific practice, personal honesty, distrust of authority, and an understanding that false explanations can cause harm. The phrase is useful because you do not need to reconstruct that whole history every time you decide whether to conceal an error.

The problem begins when the compression becomes opaque to its own origin. Then I value truth stops meaning this phrase is shorthand for a large explanatory structure and begins to mean my commitment to truth explains itself.

The same danger applies to epistemic compressions. Consider:

all life is problem solving.

That sentence could become a slogan repeated whenever difficulty appears. Instead, we decompressed it. What is a problem? How does a problem become a problem for you? How do tentative solutions arise? What counts as criticism? What happens between epistemic states? How can the future remain open if physical processes remain causally continuous?

Likewise:

evil is the lack of knowledge

could become a moral slogan. We found it useful by asking what kind of knowledge is lacking, how harmful explanations persist, why intelligence can coexist with cruelty, how explanatory closure can stabilize suffering, and what it means for a corrigible person to preserve a morally destructive explanation.

The compressed phrase became useful because the explanation remained recoverable.

Values are often harder to decompress because they arrive already elevated: revealed, received, sacred, traditional, natural, self-evident, civilizational, constitutional, foundational. Their source may be theological, political, familial, or professional. A value may also be tied to identity so deeply that it appears older than any explicit explanation you can reconstruct.

That is unsurprising. Much of your epistemic life is inexplicit. You can know how to move, notice, trust, fear, prefer, reject, or anticipate without possessing an explicit account of how that knowledge is represented. Values can occupy the same hidden landscape.

You may not be able to say why something feels sacred, why a violation appears immediately disgusting, or where a distinction entered your epistemic mind. You may have reconstructed it from family life, stories, institutions, religion, law, schooling, peers, media, private experience, and countless small criticisms distributed over decades.

The resulting value can feel immediate. That immediacy does not show that the value is foundational. It may show that the explanatory road has become invisible. You experience the destination without seeing the route.

Your epistemic mind is vast enough to hide that route. It contains memories, skills, expectations, emotional associations, categories, perceptual habits, social knowledge, language, theories, inexplicit distinctions, unfinished problems, and objective knowledge reconstructed from outside you. These structures need not be consciously available, neatly organized, or mutually consistent.

You can retain contradictions for years without experiencing them as contradictions. A contradiction does not automatically become a problem; enough tension must become salient for reconstruction to begin. A value can therefore participate in circular explanation without the circle ever becoming visible.

The simple form is:

V→E→V.V \rightarrow E \rightarrow V.V→E→V.

The lived form may be distributed:

V→A1→A2→A3→E→A4→A5→V.V \rightarrow A_1 \rightarrow A_2 \rightarrow A_3 \rightarrow E \rightarrow A_4 \rightarrow A_5 \rightarrow V.V→A1​→A2​→A3​→E→A4​→A5​→V.

The route may pass through childhood assumptions, religious concepts, social categories, emotional salience, political vocabulary, historical narratives, remembered authority, and long-inexplicit distinctions. By the time the explanation returns to the value, no circle is felt. You simply experience: of course creation requires a creator; of course truth is manifest; of course a good parent protects a child from corrupting influences; of course liberty requires intolerance of illiberalism.

The phrase of course is epistemically interesting because it can mark a transition that no longer appears as a transition. A value can feel self-evident because the arrows supporting it have disappeared into the depths of your epistemic mind.

What happens when the invisible road is arranged so that criticism itself protects the value?

2. Reflexivity Can Become Immunity

Circularity is often treated as a textbook logical defect. That description is too weak for what matters here. The more useful question is: what does the circle do?

Suppose you value creation and adopt an explanation in which creation requires a creator. The existence of creation becomes evidence of the creator, while the creator becomes the source of the value of creation. The value participates in an explanation that returns to establish the value.

Suppose instead that you value truth and possess a doctrine under which truth reveals itself to the properly oriented person. Your recognition of the doctrine becomes evidence that the doctrine is true, while failure to recognize it becomes evidence of distortion in the observer.

Or suppose you value liberty and possess an explanation under which illiberal views threaten the possibility of liberty. The existence of such views becomes a reason to restrict them, and the restriction becomes an expression of commitment to liberty. The value has authorized conduct that appears, at first glance, to oppose the value.

The contents differ. The reflexive form is similar:

V→E→interpretation of V→V.V \rightarrow E \rightarrow \text{interpretation of }V \rightarrow V.V→E→interpretation of V→V.

The problem becomes more severe when criticism itself enters the loop.

A doctrine says:

doubt is temptation.

You doubt the doctrine. The doubt does not become criticism; it becomes evidence that temptation is operating.

An ideology says:

members of the dominant class cannot recognize their own domination.

A member of the alleged class rejects the explanation. The rejection becomes evidence of the blindness being alleged.

A political doctrine says:

intolerant people exploit tolerance in order to destroy tolerance.

Someone questions whether a particular opponent should be excluded. The question becomes evidence that the critic is naively tolerating intolerance.

Abstract it.

The operation is the same:

C(V)→classification of critic→dismissal of C(V).C(V) \rightarrow \text{classification of critic} \rightarrow \text{dismissal of }C(V).C(V)→classification of critic→dismissal of C(V).

The criticism is not answered. The critic is explained. The question has changed from is this value or doctrine wrong? to what is wrong with the person who cannot see that it is right?

That is an immunizing structure. It consumes possible falsifiers. A disagreement that might have generated a successor problem becomes confirmation that the existing problem was correctly formulated. Criticism could expose a hidden assumption and create a problem that did not previously exist. Here, criticism is prevented from becoming a problem at all.

Once criticism has been converted into evidence of moral defect, classification becomes easy. The critic can be called sinful, corrupted, impure, irrational, unenlightened, hateful, intolerant, decadent, brainwashed, extremist, or dangerous. The vocabulary changes; the structure does not:

criticism of explanation→classification of person→loss of standing of criticism.\text{criticism of explanation} \rightarrow \text{classification of person} \rightarrow \text{loss of standing of criticism}.criticism of explanation→classification of person→loss of standing of criticism.

A measured output became a trait, then the trait became the person. A social category became a causal explanation. A diagnosis became a mechanism. An institutional role became an ontology.

Now a retained value becomes the person.

The moral category absorbs the explanatory agent, and the move can feel rigorous from inside the insulated structure. If obedience is virtue, disobedience is vice. If purity is virtue, contamination is vice. If tolerance is virtue, intolerance is vice. The conclusion appears deductive because the protected value has already fixed the terms of the deduction.

Abstract the structure.

The structure is symmetric. A value system you reject can perform the same operation. Suppose one side has:

VA→critics of VA are corrupt,V_A \rightarrow \text{critics of }V_A\text{ are corrupt},VA​→critics of VA​ are corrupt,

while the other has:

VB→supporters of VA are corrupt.V_B \rightarrow \text{supporters of }V_A\text{ are corrupt}.VB​→supporters of VA​ are corrupt.

One side sees decadence where the other sees oppression; one sees moral collapse where the other sees authoritarianism. The same action is reconstructed inside different epistemic worlds, and each explanation predicts the other's response. The response therefore feels confirmatory:

VA→interpret VB→VAV_A \rightarrow \text{interpret }V_B \rightarrow V_AVA​→interpret VB​→VA​

while:

VB→interpret VA→VB.V_B \rightarrow \text{interpret }V_A \rightarrow V_B.VB​→interpret VA​→VB​.

The two systems become mutually immunizing. More observations need not create more criticism because the explanatory structures are already determining what the observations mean. A problem still has to become visible.

The same architecture can scale. A protected value can support another explanation, which supports another. One part explains moral authority, another history, another dissent, another insider insight, another outsider blindness, another the danger of rival explanations. Eventually the system may contain a theory of why criticism proves the critic's corruption:

V→E1→E2→E3→E4V \rightarrow E_1 \rightarrow E_2 \rightarrow E_3 \rightarrow E_4V→E1​→E2​→E3​→E4​

while:

E4→protection of V.E_4 \rightarrow \text{protection of }V.E4​→protection of V.

A religion or ideology organized this way can contain enormous explanatory structure. The defect is not the presence of explanation. It is that criticism is interpreted from inside the system it is supposed to criticize.

Reflexivity has become immunity. Reflexivity itself is not the defect. This project is reflexive: if knowledge grows through criticism, then the epistemology must remain criticizable; if explanations have reach, the explanation of explanations must be allowed to create problems for itself; if you are fallible, so are the authors of the epistemology.

Abstract it.

Healthy reflexivity exposes the explanation to more criticism:

E→E(E)→new criticism.E \rightarrow E(E) \rightarrow \text{new criticism}.E→E(E)→new criticism.

Immunizing reflexivity protects it:

E→E(C(E))→dismiss criticism.E \rightarrow E(C(E)) \rightarrow \text{dismiss criticism}.E→E(C(E))→dismiss criticism.

The relevant distinction is therefore not linear versus circular explanation. It is reflexivity that exposes versus reflexivity that protects.

Once a value can protect itself this way, its moral status can be transferred to whatever contains it.

3. The Logic of Contamination

Suppose a value has already been classified as morally bad. A further compression becomes possible: whatever contains the value inherits its status.

Vbad∈X→Xbad.V_{\text{bad}}\in X \rightarrow X_{\text{bad}}.Vbad​∈X→Xbad​.

The carrier disappears into the classification. A person who contains the value becomes a bad person. An artifact that contains it becomes a bad artifact. A technology that embodies or transmits it becomes a bad technology.

The same move can be written three ways:

person contains bad value→reject person,\text{person contains bad value} \rightarrow \text{reject person},person contains bad value→reject person, artifact contains bad value→reject artifact,\text{artifact contains bad value} \rightarrow \text{reject artifact},artifact contains bad value→reject artifact, technology embodies or transmits bad value→reject technology.\text{technology embodies or transmits bad value} \rightarrow \text{reject technology}.technology embodies or transmits bad value→reject technology.

In each case, the containing structure loses its complexity. A person becomes the explanation they presently retain. A book becomes the doctrine it contains. A platform becomes the worst idea transmitted through it. A technology becomes the harmful use made of it.

Once the objectionable value is detected, it detonates the moral standing of the whole. That transfer will be called the value bomb.

The name matters because the mechanism recurs. Suppose an artifact contains an explanation judged morally unacceptable. Under a pollution model, reconstruction of the whole artifact is unnecessary; the objectionable value is enough:

Vbad→Kbad.V_{\text{bad}} \rightarrow K_{\text{bad}}.Vbad​→Kbad​.

If you then encounter the artifact, the same model can treat the value as though it transfers directly:

Kbad→Ppolluted.K_{\text{bad}} \rightarrow P_{\text{polluted}}.Kbad​→Ppolluted​.

You become a carrier. If you speak, the value may spread further. An epistemic problem has been turned into a sanitation problem.

The vocabulary follows: exposure, contamination, corruption, toxicity, radicalization, dangerous content, unsafe ideas, harmful influence. Some of these words can refer to real causal processes. People can be manipulated. Propaganda can be destructive. Your epistemic mind can become worse. Bad explanations can spread.

The pollution model becomes misleading when those causal processes are replaced by passive transfer. You are no longer reconstructing, criticizing, misunderstanding, rejecting, modifying, or integrating objective knowledge. You are absorbing a substance. That is the old bucket theory in moral form.

The architecture predates digital technology. We encountered it in the history of media. A new medium gives people access to forms of objective knowledge that previous institutions did not fully control. Some of that knowledge is poor, manipulative, morally abhorrent, or merely unfamiliar. The status of the content is then transferred to the medium:

bad content→bad medium→restrict medium.\text{bad content} \rightarrow \text{bad medium} \rightarrow \text{restrict medium}.bad content→bad medium→restrict medium.

The same pattern can recur with books, popular music, television, games, the internet, smartphones, social media, and artificial intelligence. The surface changes, but the medium remains the moral carrier of the value.

Technological abstinence can therefore feel like an explanation: remove the carrier, remove the pollution, protect the person. That compression is already difficult to sustain.

4. Technology and the Apparatus of Preservation

The category technology is already too broad to function within an explanation. Technology has appeared in several different relations: as objective knowledge embodied in artifacts; as a medium that extends memory, communication, coordination, and criticism; and as an instrument through which surveillance, manipulation, censorship, or ideological amplification can also occur.

Those are not contradictory descriptions. They are different uses and mechanisms carried by a broad technological substrate. The existence of malicious uses therefore cannot, by itself, settle the moral status of the category technology.

Artificial intelligence makes the symmetry unusually visible. AI can reinforce a value, restate it persuasively, help construct a coherent ideological explanation, classify speech according to an inherited framework, or suppress rival explanations. The same broad technology can also decompress a slogan, reconstruct assumptions, compare explanatory structures, expose circularity, retrieve criticism, and preserve a long explanatory surface that unaided memory would compress.

The category does not settle the local problem. The same system can participate in closure or decompression. Calling AI morally neutral in this limited sense does not imply that every system or use is equally good or bad. It means that the explanation cannot stop at AI.

The structure becomes especially visible when the person being protected is a child because the authority relation is already established.

A parent possesses some explanation of a good childhood. It may include physical play, direct relationships, attention, nature, books, local community, family continuity, protection from commercial manipulation, or protection from explicit content. Those concerns may be real and may support good local restrictions.

Once compressed, however, the explanation can become:

I value a low-technology childhood.

That sentence can conceal many different causal claims. The problem begins when the value substitutes for the claims it compressed:

low technology is good→technology is harmful→restriction is good.\text{low technology is good} \rightarrow \text{technology is harmful} \rightarrow \text{restriction is good}.low technology is good→technology is harmful→restriction is good.

Now the distinctions that matter disappear. Which technology? Used how? For what problem? By which child? Under which social conditions? What knowledge becomes available? What criticism becomes unavailable? What changes when a restriction removes a harmful interaction, and what changes when it removes access to an explanation the parent does not possess?

Those questions reopen the compression.

A parent can lose epistemic overlap with a child as the child develops interests, relationships, vocabulary, and knowledge inside environments the parent does not understand. The parent's experienced problem may be:

I no longer understand this part of my child's world.

Yet a restriction can be described as solving a different problem:

this part of my child's world is dangerous.

Values can conceal that substitution. A parent who values a low-technology childhood may have a good explanation. The same restriction may also preserve an explanatory environment in which the adult's values remain comparatively uncontested.

That is not a universal diagnosis of parents. It identifies a structural question: does the restriction solve a demonstrated problem for the child, or does it also preserve the adult's preferred epistemic landscape by controlling which rival explanations become available?

The same structure produces a revealing symmetry at the level of technology. Technology is often described as the source of polluted values: the internet spreads extremist ideology, social media radicalizes, AI produces harmful content. Yet the proposed remedies are often technological too: filtering, content moderation, parental controls, automated classification, identity systems, surveillance, or AI safety systems.

So the same variable appears on both sides:

T→pollutionT \rightarrow \text{pollution}T→pollution

and:

T→purification.T \rightarrow \text{purification}.T→purification.

The apparent contradiction disappears once the category is decompressed. Technology is not the polluter or the purifier. People are using objective knowledge to influence which objective knowledge other people encounter. The moral disagreement occurs through the technology rather than residing inside it as an essence.

Modern filtering systems are extensions of an older project. Human societies have long created objective knowledge that preserves values across generations: religious canons, schools, professional codes, constitutions, censorship regimes, curricula, rituals, libraries, archives, and institutions of interpretation. These structures can preserve good explanations. They can also preserve immunized values.

Digital systems alter the scale and responsiveness of the apparatus. A filter can operate continuously. A classifier can intervene before a rival explanation is encountered. A system can personalize what becomes visible. AI can increase the granularity further.

The underlying relation is older:

V→objective knowledge→preservation of V.V \rightarrow \text{objective knowledge} \rightarrow \text{preservation of }V.V→objective knowledge→preservation of V.

Preservation is not itself the defect. The problem begins when preservation becomes insulation from criticism.

Once rival values are treated as pollution, protecting people begins to mean managing exposure. Some cases involve concrete causal problems: a child can be protected from an adult attempting manipulation; a person can be protected from threats; an institution can establish boundaries needed for its function.

The pollution model expands when the rival explanation itself becomes the danger:

bad explanation→bad objective knowledge→restricted access.\text{bad explanation} \rightarrow \text{bad objective knowledge} \rightarrow \text{restricted access}.bad explanation→bad objective knowledge→restricted access.

Applied to adults, the model creates an old asymmetry. An authority is assumed competent to encounter the bad explanation and classify it, while the ordinary person is assumed to require protection from it. The authority may examine the poison; the public should not. That is the authority memeplex again.

The same problem reappears inside liberalism, where values created to preserve liberty can themselves become insulated from criticism.

5. Liberal Values After the Explanation

We did not begin with liberal declarations: liberty is a Western value, free speech is a liberal principle, or pluralism is good because liberal democracy says so. Instead, we reconstructed their problems.

You are fallible. No authority possesses complete knowledge. Criticism requires access to alternatives. Coercion can prevent criticism from entering your problem situation. Objective knowledge also lets persons with very different internal worlds coordinate through relatively thin interfaces without requiring deep agreement. Political structures that permit criticism, exit, revision, dissent, and nonviolent removal of bad policies therefore solve important epistemic problems.

Only after that reconstruction do words such as liberty, tolerance, pluralism, free speech, and democracy become useful compressions. The labels are not the explanatory grounds. They are what remains after the explanation has been compressed. That matters because liberalism can become immunized in the same way as any other doctrine if its compressed values are allowed to reverse the explanatory sequence.

The phrase paradox of tolerance makes the danger visible. The familiar formulation is that a tolerant society may be destroyed if it tolerates people who would abolish tolerance, and therefore tolerance requires intolerance toward intolerance.

The problem described by the slogan is real. A society that permits open criticism may encounter people who advocate or undertake actions that would eliminate those conditions.

But the problem does not contain its own solution. A tentative solution still has to be created and exposed to criticism:

How can institutions preserve the conditions of criticism when some people advocate or undertake actions that would destroy those conditions?

That formulation does not settle the case in advance. Different problems may support different tentative solutions: counter-speech, constitutional structure, exit rights, institutional boundaries, rules against coercive conduct, legal protections, procedural constraints, or further criticism.

A specific restriction may solve a specific problem. The phrase paradox of tolerance cannot perform that explanatory work by itself.

The danger appears when the problem is compressed into a rule:

intolerance→must not be tolerated.\text{intolerance} \rightarrow \text{must not be tolerated}.intolerance→must not be tolerated.

At once, another problem appears. What counts as intolerance? Who classifies it? Which actions are being prohibited? Which explanations are being excluded? What criticism would change the classification?

If those questions disappear, the slogan has ceased to identify a problem and has become a jurisdiction:

classified intolerance→exclusion permitted.\text{classified intolerance} \rightarrow \text{exclusion permitted}.classified intolerance→exclusion permitted.

The immunizing structure then returns. Someone questions whether exclusion is warranted, and the question is recoded as evidence of moral failure:

C→tolerance of bad value→bad critic.C \rightarrow \text{tolerance of bad value} \rightarrow \text{bad critic}.C→tolerance of bad value→bad critic.

The value bomb is now operating inside a doctrine meant to preserve criticism. To question whether the bomb has been correctly identified is treated as willingness to tolerate the explosion. That is precisely the structure liberal institutions were supposed to keep open to criticism.

The abstraction intolerance is too broad to settle local cases. Political campaigns try to change other people's values. Religious movements evangelize. Activists condemn institutions. Parents transmit moral beliefs. Editors reject submissions. Universities and professional associations impose standards. Friends tell one another that certain conduct is unacceptable.

Many of these activities can be called intolerant in an ordinary sense. The label alone therefore does not distinguish among them. The local explanation has to recover the causal problem: what action occurred, what constraint was imposed, what criticism became impossible, what institution is being protected, what harm is being prevented, and what alternative solution exists.

That distinction also separates explanation from conduct. Criticizing the pollution model does not entail that every action inspired by every value must be permitted. A person can threaten, use violence, advocate coercion, manipulate access to resources, or suppress criticism through force. Those are concrete causal problems.

The epistemic distinction is narrower:

bad explanation≠permission for every conduct inspired by it\text{bad explanation} \neq \text{permission for every conduct inspired by it}bad explanation=permission for every conduct inspired by it

and:

bad explanation≠automatic warrant to exclude every carrier of it.\text{bad explanation} \neq \text{automatic warrant to exclude every carrier of it}.bad explanation=automatic warrant to exclude every carrier of it.

The moral classification of an explanation does not by itself explain the causal problem created by an action, institution, artifact, or technology. Reconstructing that problem is less convenient than applying a value rule, but it preserves the fallibility that gave the liberal values their explanatory force in the first place.

If values are not foundations, what grounds the explanation that produced them?

6. No Final Ground

The demand for a ground has a familiar form. If values are not foundations, what grounds morality? Liberty? Criticism? The goodness of an explanation?

We encounter the same architecture in justificationism. The demand assumes that some deeper structure must convert a conjecture into an entitled belief. If values are not the ground, perhaps reason is. If not reason, perhaps evidence; if not evidence, consensus; if not consensus, probability; if not probability, the epistemology itself. The search moves the ground downward without changing the architecture.

The epistemology employed here rejects that architecture. A problem exists. A tentative solution is created. Criticism exposes errors. A successor problem appears:

P1→TS→EE→P2.P_1 \rightarrow TS \rightarrow EE \rightarrow P_2.P1​→TS→EE→P2​.

No justification enters the sequence. That includes the epistemology itself.

It would be a mistake to exempt criticism from the argument:

all values are conjectural, except the value of criticism.

Or:

the supreme moral value is preserving error correction.

That would only install another sacred premise.

The narrower claim is conditional. If the evolutionary explanation of knowledge is approximately correct, criticism is part of the process through which errors become visible. A structure that systematically prevents criticism therefore interferes with the process described by the explanation.

That is a consequence of the explanation, not a revelation, axiom, or final ground. The epistemology can itself be criticized. A better explanation of knowledge growth would alter the consequences.

Still, conjectural explanations have consequences. If persons are fallible and corrigible, institutions that preserve opportunities for correction solve an important problem. Freedom of criticism, access to alternatives, possibilities of exit, institutional revision, changes of mind, and encounters with rival objective knowledge all follow from that explanatory structure.

Together they form something like a thin moral lattice. The phrase names a limited network of consequences, not a foundation beneath them.

Its thinness matters. A conjectural fragment can be inserted into the lattice and then protected by its position. For example:

criticism must remain possible\text{criticism must remain possible}criticism must remain possible

can become:

my preferred account of legitimate criticism must be preserved.\text{my preferred account of legitimate criticism must be preserved}.my preferred account of legitimate criticism must be preserved.

Likewise:

liberty helps preserve error correction\text{liberty helps preserve error correction}liberty helps preserve error correction

can become:

this doctrine of liberty is beyond criticism.\text{this doctrine of liberty is beyond criticism}.this doctrine of liberty is beyond criticism.

The framework built to preserve error correction can then destroy error correction from within. Liberal values cannot be their own grounds. Their explanatory ancestry has to remain available.

The remaining question is practical without requiring a final foundation: what does it look like to use a value while keeping its ancestry recoverable?

7. Reversible Compression

There is no direct access to reality through which moral truth simply appears. You do not perceive liberty is good in the way you perceive light or pain. Even perception is already mediated through evolved and learned structures. Values are no exception.

You encounter a problem. A contradiction becomes salient. An institution causes suffering. A restriction prevents criticism. A social practice produces domination. A technological possibility reveals that an inherited scarcity was unnecessary. A relationship exposes an assumption. A child asks a question. A rival explanation arrives.

Then a tentative solution becomes possible:

P→TS.P \rightarrow TS.P→TS.

Some explanations remain local. Others reorganize many problems at once. An explanation of freedom can alter politics, education, parenting, institutional design, and interpersonal relationships. An explanation of truth can alter science, journalism, friendship, law, and self-criticism. When an explanation acquires enough reach, giving it a short name makes repeated use easier.

That is the useful operation. An explanation is compressed into a value:

P→E→V.P \rightarrow E \rightarrow V.P→E→V.

The value can then guide a local action without requiring the whole explanation to be reconstructed every time:

V→local action.V \rightarrow \text{local action}.V→local action.

The compression becomes dangerous only when this shortcut is mistaken for a foundation. When a new problem appears, the route back has to remain possible:

V→E→EE.V \rightarrow E \rightarrow EE.V→E→EE.

The value is useful while the explanation it compresses can return under criticism. That relation will be called reversible compression.

Once the explanation returns, the local problem becomes visible again. Which problem was the value helping to solve? Does that problem still exist? Has the environment changed? Does the value now conflict with another explanation? Does the local application preserve the reach of the original explanation, or only repeat its label? What criticism would expose a contradiction?

A value that can survive that decompression may remain useful. A value that blocks it has become an immunizing structure.

Now the argument turns back to you, the singular person.

You may retain a terrible explanation. You may preserve a value that stabilizes cruelty. You may belong to an ideology whose internal structure makes criticism difficult. You may reject explanations we regard as among the best things civilization has created. That matters. But you are still not the value.

The value is one structure inside an epistemic mind much larger than the surface available to anyone else. And even that world is not the future you. A new problem can become visible. A criticism can arrive from an unexpected direction. A value that once felt foundational can itself become the object of inquiry.

The value bomb destroys that openness when it compresses:

bad value→bad person.\text{bad value} \rightarrow \text{bad person}.bad value→bad person.

The present epistemic state becomes an ontology, first in psychology. It has now reappeared in morality.

Objective knowledge is larger than the value too. A book can contain a bad explanation. A historical document can preserve cruelty. A political text can contain hierarchy, exclusion, or error. Yet the artifact can later become material for criticism. An explanation that once persuaded can become the example through which another generation understands how persuasion failed. An archive of error can become part of error elimination.

So:

contains bad explanation\text{contains bad explanation}contains bad explanation

does not entail:

has no epistemic value.\text{has no epistemic value}.has no epistemic value.

Technology completes the parallel. A technological system may embody bad knowledge, be built for coercion, amplify prejudice, make propaganda cheaper, or enable surveillance. Those are real criticisms. They do not make the category technology explanatory enough to settle the case.

The same broad substrate may also permit communication, criticism, coordination, discovery, creativity, and new problem formation. The relevant causal questions therefore remain local: which technology, which use, which person, which problem, which mechanism, which suppressed alternative, which criticism would change the conclusion?

Those questions do not refute the value. They decompress it. And once the value has been decompressed, the constraint of the open future becomes unavoidable.

8. The Open Future

Future knowledge cannot already be latent if it is genuinely new. That conclusion reaches values directly. A value may have enormous reach. It may summarize one of the best explanations presently available. It may organize a life, guide an institution, or preserve knowledge worth carrying forward.

It still cannot specify the future. A later criticism may reveal a problem we cannot presently imagine. A new technology may alter the action space. A new moral explanation may expose an inherited cruelty. You may discover that two values you treated as compatible depend upon contradictory explanations. No present value can therefore contain the knowledge that does not yet exist, and no present value gets jurisdiction over it.

That does not imply moral passivity. It locates the value inside fallibility.

The practical use of a value is then less mysterious. A compressed explanation can coordinate action, preserve hard-won knowledge, and travel farther than the full argument that produced it. But when a new problem appears, the compression can reopen.

That return also preserves you, the person. A present value can guide action without becoming the ontology of the person who retains it. Objective knowledge can contain error without becoming identical to the error. Technology can carry a harmful use without becoming identical to the use.

The common structure is the refusal to let the moral status of one compressed explanation detonate the larger thing that contains it.

The architecture can now be stated without turning it into another foundation. A problem gives rise to an explanation. The explanation can be compressed into a value and reused in action:

P→E→V→action.P \rightarrow E \rightarrow V \rightarrow \text{action}.P→E→V→action.

When criticism creates a new problem, the compression can reopen:

V→E→EE→P2.V \rightarrow E \rightarrow EE \rightarrow P_2.V→E→EE→P2​.

The value is not a foundation. It is a compression. The explanation remains conjectural.

And you remain larger than either. But the ability to criticize, revise, test, and replace an explanation is not free. It takes time, tools, access to objective knowledge, other people, and physical resources.

What happens when persons are epistemically equal in kind but radically unequal in the resources through which their knowledge can become consequential?
