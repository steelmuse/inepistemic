The Extended Epistemic Mind

XXII. The Maps Become The Territory

The world is becoming increasingly abstract, more quickly than any person can reconstruct it. Technology, institutions, automation, law, money, software, markets, science, and artificial intelligence accumulate objective knowledge outside any one mind. You increasingly act through interfaces whose underlying causal structures you cannot explicitly carry.

The same objective knowledge that enlarges human reach can magnify error. A bad explanation can remain private. It can also become a law, a financial structure, a software system, a safety doctrine, a bureaucracy, a technological standard, or a machine-executable instruction. The larger the causal reach of the abstraction, the larger the possible error.

Here we explore that symmetry from within your vast and largely inexplicit mind.

There is one reality. But you encounter it through an evolving structure of perception, memory, expectation, language, concepts, values, explanations, skills, technologies, institutions, and inherited objective knowledge. Everyone else encounters the same reality through a history that differs from yours.

That is miraculous. There are billions of people. As a network of epistemic minds, society should be unimaginably complicated. Yet ordinary life often does not feel that way. You meet, trade, drive, board airplanes, send money, go to school, sign contracts, use software, buy food from strangers, and work with people whose inner worlds you barely know. You coordinate at scales you cannot explicitly reconstruct.

That looks like a problem for the thesis of radical epistemic individuality. If the worlds inside people are really that different, how does any of this work?

The complexity has not disappeared. It has been compressed.

Society increasingly coordinates through objective abstractions that allow radically different minds to act together without requiring those minds to become alike. That is the abstract society: not a society detached from reality, not a collective mind, and not a world of mere symbols, but a social world in which an increasing share of coordination occurs through objective abstractions whose underlying causal and epistemic structures need not be reconstructed by the people using them.

That arrangement has a peculiar consequence. The world can become more abstract while your local experience becomes simpler. The same abstractions can enlarge your freedom and your dependency, preserve knowledge and preserve error, make deep difference compatible with practical coordination, and conceal the assumptions through which one person's world is projected onto another.

The abstract society is therefore not merely a technological condition. It is another expression of the epistemic problem.

1. The Narrow Interface

Represent the social epistemic situation very crudely as:

M1,M2,M3,…,MnM_1, M_2, M_3, \dots, M_nM1​,M2​,M3​,…,Mn​

where each MiM_iMi​ is the evolving epistemic mind of one person.

For billions of people:

n≈8 billion.n \approx 8 \text{ billion}.n≈8 billion.

The notation hides almost everything important. Each MiM_iMi​ contains explicit and inexplicit knowledge accumulated through a unique life: abstractions built from earlier abstractions, memories whose significance depends on other memories, moral concepts whose meaning depends on explanations that may never have been articulated, skills that cannot be fully stated, inherited cultural structures, errors, and contradictions that may not yet have become problems.

The contents also change one another. You do not carry a fixed inventory of beliefs. One retained explanation changes which later problems become visible. One humiliation can alter later salience. One scientific explanation can reorganize what you notice. One new value can recast decades of memory. One technology can make previously unavailable problems available. A child can transform a parent's problem world.

The internal space is therefore not merely large. It is historically evolved and recursive.

If that description is even approximately right, the combined social epistemic landscape is beyond explicit representation. Yet a cashier and a customer can complete an exchange in seconds. A pilot can transport hundreds of strangers. A hospital can coordinate thousands of people with different goals and specializations. A city can continue functioning while most of its residents remain strangers.

From this vantage point, the social world looks much thinner than the minds composing it. One possible explanation would be that successful coordination reveals deeper mental convergence than the thesis allows. But ordinary cases point in another direction. Two people can use the same word while reconstructing different meanings. Two parties can sign the same contract for different reasons. Two workers can complete the same process while explaining its purpose differently. Two drivers can obey the same traffic signal while inhabiting radically different moral, political, familial, and conceptual worlds.

Coordination therefore does not require epistemic merger.

It can instead be represented as:

Mi→I←MjM_i \rightarrow I \leftarrow M_jMi​→I←Mj​

where III is an interface.

The interface does not merge the minds. It creates a narrow structure through which their actions become mutually compatible. A traffic signal does not require drivers to agree about transportation policy. A payment terminal does not require a customer and merchant to share a theory of money. A contract does not require both parties to possess the same conception of justice.

The interface specifies enough. Civilization scales partly by reducing how much of another person's internal world you must reconstruct before you can coordinate with them. The success of that coordination is not evidence that the internal difference is small. It is evidence that objective abstractions can bridge large differences cheaply.

That gives the first compression. The immense social problem is not solved by making minds alike. It is made tractable by narrowing what must pass between them.

2. Compression Builds the Abstract Society

An abstraction does two things at once. It creates a new layer of complexity, then suppresses the need to encounter much of the complexity is displaced.

Consider sending a short message. The act may depend on semiconductor physics, operating systems, networking protocols, cryptography, radio systems, fiber optics, software libraries, corporate infrastructure, legal agreements, language, writing, and social conventions.

You experience:

send.

Paying for a coffee may depend on monetary institutions, accounting, payment networks, identity systems, software, taxation, property rules, and supply chains.

You experience:

tap.

The local action becomes simple because a much deeper causal structure has already been compressed into an interface. So:

greater underlying abstraction⇏greater experienced complexity.\text{greater underlying abstraction} \not\Rightarrow \text{greater experienced complexity}.greater underlying abstraction⇒greater experienced complexity.

Sometimes:

greater underlying abstraction→simpler local action.\text{greater underlying abstraction} \rightarrow \text{simpler local action}.greater underlying abstraction→simpler local action.

That is why abstract society often does not feel abstract. Its abstractions succeed partly by becoming experientially quiet. The causal depth remains; you are relieved of the need to reconstruct it.

The same structure appears beyond technology. Society can be understood as an evolving architecture for compressing otherwise unmanageable epistemic complexity into objective forms that different people can use. Language, writing, numbers, money, maps, property systems, contracts, law, professional credentials, technical standards, organizational roles, constitutions, software, and databases all perform versions of this operation. Markets sometimes do. Artificial intelligence can do it dynamically.

The common relation matters more than the list. Each abstraction converts some complicated problem of coordination into a narrower interface. A role such as nurse, judge, driver, accountant, teacher, or engineer does not contain the person. It compresses expectations about a limited region of coordination. A dollar does not contain a theory of value. It permits exchange without reconstructing the entire history of what each party wants. A property title does not reproduce every physical relation between a person and a place. It creates an objective structure around which claims can be coordinated.

A corporation makes the point especially clear. It does not become a person merely because law treats it as an entity for some purposes. The abstraction compresses relationships among people, capital, contracts, obligations, and decision procedures.

That boundary matters, but the distinction needs to be precise. Networks of people, institutions, technologies, and accumulated objective knowledge can preserve information, transform it, scale it, and produce emergent effects that no individual intended or explicitly reconstructed. Those properties can reasonably be described as mind-like, or even as a kind of social mind if you prefer. Nothing important here depends on the name.

The epistemic claim is narrower. The epistemic mind is the locus in which problems become problems, explanations are created, criticism is understood, and new conjectures arise. Those capacities are presently located in people. Objective knowledge can persist outside them, networks can reorganize it, institutions can scale it, and emergent structures can acquire causal powers unavailable to any individual participant. But none of those facts yet explains a non-personal locus of explanatory creativity.

The distinction is therefore not between a society that may or may not be called a mind and an individual that unquestionably is one. It is between the objective structures through which knowledge persists and scales, and the still-unexplained epistemic capacities through which new explanations are created.

The historical development of these abstractions is complicated and cannot be reduced to a single linear progression. A broad direction is nevertheless visible: coordination can occur at increasing distance from direct physical familiarity.

Face-to-face knowledge permits local coordination. Language extends coordination beyond immediate perception. Writing lets knowledge persist beyond the presence of the speaker. Numbers detach quantitative relations from particular objects. Money permits exchange among people who need not know one another's larger purposes. Law stabilizes recurring expectations across strangers. Technical standards let artifacts created by different people interoperate. Software turns explicit knowledge into repeatable executable structure. Global networks let abstractions move with little regard to physical distance. Artificial intelligence increasingly lets you interrogate and reorganize large stores of objective knowledge through a conversational interface.

Abstract it.

The broad direction can be represented approximately as:

direct coordination→symbolic coordination→persistent objective knowledge→institutional abstraction→technological abstraction→dynamic interfaces to abstraction.\text{direct coordination} \rightarrow \text{symbolic coordination} \rightarrow \text{persistent objective knowledge} \rightarrow \text{institutional abstraction} \rightarrow \text{technological abstraction} \rightarrow \text{dynamic interfaces to abstraction}.direct coordination→symbolic coordination→persistent objective knowledge→institutional abstraction→technological abstraction→dynamic interfaces to abstraction.

This is not an account of inevitable progress. The same capacity that preserves knowledge can preserve terrible explanations. Institutions can become rigid. Writing can preserve dogma. Law can embody cruelty. Software can scale injustice. Artificial intelligence can make coercion cheaper.

The historical claim is narrower: civilization increasingly operates through layers of objective abstraction that later people can use without reconstructing how those layers were created.

Then another transition occurs. The layers themselves become objects of later abstraction.

A number can represent physical objects. An accounting system can represent numbers. A financial instrument can represent claims represented inside an accounting system. Software can operate software. A model can evaluate models. An institution can regulate institutions. An artificial intelligence system can operate on a library containing centuries of prior abstractions and return a new compressed representation to you, which you can then use to create another explanation.

Abstract it again.

The external world therefore develops recursive structure:

A1(R)→A2(A1)→A3(A2)→…A_1(R) \rightarrow A_2(A_1) \rightarrow A_3(A_2) \rightarrow \dotsA1​(R)→A2​(A1​)→A3​(A2​)→…

where each AkA_kAk​ can become material for later abstraction.

This resembles the recursive abstraction already explored inside your individual epistemic mind, but the resemblance should not collapse the distinction. People externalize knowledge into objective knowledge. Those structures enter networks, interact with other abstractions, become material for later transformations, and can produce emergent effects at scales no individual designed or explicitly understands.

One may call the resulting organization a social mind. The label does not answer the epistemic question. What remains to be explained is whether the larger structure contains the capacity under examination here: encountering a problem as a problem, creating a new explanation, understanding criticism of it, and creating another conjecture in response.

The recursion is social. The objective knowledge is distributed. Its effects can be emergent. But the presently unexplained capacity for explanatory creativity remains located in persons.

That distinction prepares the turn into your own situation. If society coordinates by hiding more and more causal depth behind usable interfaces, what happens to you when your available action depends on structures you cannot reconstruct?

3. What Abstraction Does to You

The first effect is enabling. You do not need to understand electrical generation to use a lamp. You do not need to understand monetary policy to buy lunch. You do not need to understand semiconductor fabrication to write a document. You do not need to reconstruct the history of contract law to accept ordinary terms of service. You do not need to understand the complete causal stack behind an airplane to travel across an ocean.

This is not praise of ignorance. It is a consequence of successful objective knowledge. Abstraction lets you inherit the work of countless others without reproducing that work inside your own explicit understanding.

That inheritance expands your action space:

objective knowledge→lower reconstruction burden→greater available action.\text{objective knowledge} \rightarrow \text{lower reconstruction burden} \rightarrow \text{greater available action}.objective knowledge→lower reconstruction burden→greater available action.

You can participate in transformations whose full causal structure exceeds your explicit understanding. That is one of civilization's great achievements.

The same mechanism also creates dependence. You become capable because the abstraction works. You become vulnerable because you cannot always reconstruct why it works, when it fails, who controls it, or which assumptions it contains.

The same abstraction can therefore produce:

freedom\text{freedom}freedom

and:

dependency.\text{dependency}.dependency.

The two are not competing descriptions. The freedom is enabled by the dependency. You can act without reconstruction because the abstraction carries knowledge for you; that is also why failure or distortion inside the abstraction can exceed what you can immediately diagnose.

The dependence becomes harder to see when the interface becomes ordinary. A functioning abstraction can become so familiar that its mediating role disappears. This is the social manifestation of manifest truth. You no longer experience yourself as using one historically developed interface among several possible interfaces. You experience: this is how things are.

That shift is small in experience and large in consequence. A credit score can become creditworthiness. A professional credential can become competence. A school grade can become ability. A market price can become value. A medical code can become the patient. A policy category can become the citizen.

The abstraction may track something important. The problem begins when the representation stops being experienced as a representation and becomes an explanation of the person.

Abstract society can reproduce this error institutionally. Institutions cannot carry the full epistemic life of every person, so they compress. Once the compression is forgotten, however, the representation can begin governing the person it only partially represents.

That changes your practical position inside society. If more social action passes through abstraction, then facility with the relevant abstractions affects which parts of society you can actually navigate. This does not create a hierarchy of persons. It creates an asymmetry in access to present interfaces.

You may be a universal explainer and still find a tax system impossible to navigate. A brilliant craftsperson may be defeated by a benefits portal. A patient may understand their own suffering while being unable to translate it into the categories required by an institution.

Compare it.

The relation is:

facility with relevant abstraction→larger navigable social action space.\text{facility with relevant abstraction} \rightarrow \text{larger navigable social action space}.facility with relevant abstraction→larger navigable social action space.

It is not:

facility with abstraction→greater person.\text{facility with abstraction} \rightarrow \text{greater person}.facility with abstraction→greater person.

The distinction matters because abstract society can create exclusion without an explicit prohibition. No one needs to say you are forbidden. The barrier can instead appear as a form, a contract, a disclosure, a portal, a policy, a model, or a software configuration that must be understood before the transformation becomes available.

The opportunity can therefore be formally open while the path remains abstractly inaccessible. That is a real disadvantage. It does not imply inferior personhood. It means that important transformations have been placed behind abstraction layers that some people can navigate more easily than others.

The same structure can appear inside a family. A child can be physically near while occupying regions of objective abstract society that a parent cannot reconstruct. Online communities, games with elaborate internal economies, digital identities, rapidly changing language, algorithmically organized media, artificial intelligence, and technical systems can acquire meanings through layers the parent has never learned to navigate.

The distance then has two components:

internal epistemic divergence+external abstraction divergence.\text{internal epistemic divergence} + \text{external abstraction divergence}.internal epistemic divergence+external abstraction divergence.

A parent may try to restore legibility by limiting the device, banning the game, dismissing the language, rejecting the online relationship, or returning the child to activities whose meanings the parent can reconstruct. Sometimes the concern will be correct. Children encounter genuine harms. Some abstractions are bad. Some technologies exploit attention. Some communities preserve destructive explanations.

But inability to reconstruct the child's world is not itself an explanation of danger. Abstract distance can feel like moral distance. The feeling can be real while the inference remains open to criticism.

4. Norms, Projection, and Moral Compression

Formal institutions are not the only interfaces through which society coordinates. Much coordination is inexplicit. You learn how close to stand, when to speak, how loudly, whether to shake hands, when to make eye contact, how to queue, when to apologize, what counts as rude, how directly to disagree, and when silence means respect rather than contempt.

These patterns compress coordination knowledge into behavior. You do not reconstruct the history of greeting conventions before saying hello. You simply greet. The efficiency is useful because the norm carries prior coordination work into the present. But that same compression can hide the problem the norm was solving and the assumptions it contains.

A norm is therefore not reality. It is a tentative social solution that may still solve its problem, may solve it only for some people, may have outlived its original problem, or may now create problems of its own.

Abstract it.

When conflict makes the compression visible, it can be reopened:

norm→problem it was solving→assumptions→consequences→criticism.\text{norm} \rightarrow \text{problem it was solving} \rightarrow \text{assumptions} \rightarrow \text{consequences} \rightarrow \text{criticism}.norm→problem it was solving→assumptions→consequences→criticism.

That operation is reversible compression: a compact social rule can be expanded back into the problem, assumptions, and consequences that gave it meaning.

The social world is full of such inexplicit contracts. Do not cut the line. Do not touch without permission. Keep your promises. Respect privacy. Be grateful. Respect your elders. Support your family. Tell the truth. Do not embarrass the group.

Some protect important knowledge. Some conflict. Some depend on context. Some are artifacts of hierarchy. Some become cruel when applied universally.

Their inexplicitness does not make them unreal. A child can learn a rule before being able to explain it. You can feel shame after violating one without knowing what problem the rule originally solved. A community can punish deviation while lacking an explicit account of why the rule exists. Social norms are therefore objective abstractions operating partly through inexplicit knowledge. They are real, useful, and fallible.

The next difficulty appears when a useful interface carries an implicit model of the people using it. A heterogeneous society needs narrow protocols precisely because people differ. The more difference a social system can tolerate, the more coordination can be carried by interfaces that demand little agreement about the inner person.

A criticizable interface therefore has a recognizable structure. Its jurisdiction is limited. Coercion remains relatively low where the coordination problem permits it. Refusal remains possible. Arrangements remain reversible where possible. Harmless variation does not automatically become defect. Misunderstanding can be corrected. Changed circumstances can reopen the rule.

This does not turn every interaction into explicit negotiation. That would destroy the efficiency the norm provides. The distinction is narrower: when the interface fails, the person need not automatically become the defect. The abstraction itself can become the problem.

The recurring criterion is the possibility of error correction: does the structure remain open to criticism when the compression fails? That is the first moral consequence of the compression architecture. The second appears when the abstraction tells you how another person ought to be treated.

Consider the Golden Rule:

Do unto others as you would have them do unto you.

Its moral intention is understandable. It asks you to escape narrow self-interest by imagining the position of another person.

But the rule still begins with you:

my preferred treatment→your treatment.\text{my preferred treatment} \rightarrow \text{your treatment}.my preferred treatment→your treatment.

That relation assumes a symmetry you do not possess.

Suppose you enjoy friendly conversation with strangers. You smile at a cashier, make eye contact, say hello enthusiastically, perhaps offer a handshake. You are giving the other person what you would welcome from them.

The same behavior may be invasive inside the other person's problem situation. They may dislike eye contact, find informal conversation exhausting, experience touch as threatening, or be occupied by a private problem you cannot see. Nothing about your intention settles their experience.

Reverse the case. Suppose you value distance and prefer to complete transactions without social performance, so you offer others the same courtesy. A customer says hello; you look away; the customer experiences contempt. You may believe you are respecting the boundary you would want respected.

Both actors can follow the Golden Rule and still produce a social failure. The problem is not kindness. It is projected symmetry: a model built from your own preferred treatment is being used to infer another person's good.

A negative formulation appears to reduce the problem:

Do not do unto others what you would not have them do unto you.

The difference is real. The positive rule begins with a theory of the good treatment to provide. The negative rule begins with restraint and therefore requires less positive knowledge of the other person's inner world.

But the self remains the model. If you dislike interruption, you may infer that another person never wants to be interrupted. If you dislike unsolicited help, you may withhold help from someone who wants it. If you dislike physical affection, you may deny affection to someone for whom it is important.

The negative rule is therefore an epistemic improvement without becoming a moral law. It reduces one kind of projection while leaving the deeper problem intact.

The deeper principle is less elegant because it carries the uncertainty explicitly:

Do not presume that another person's good can be derived from your own. Minimize avoidable imposition, remain attentive to criticism, and revise the model when the other person's problem situation becomes clearer.

This is not a complete interpersonal code. It can't be. It is just a way of keeping the model of the other person corrigible.

The same problem can be attacked by changing the information available to the rule-maker. The veil of ignorance does this. Instead of asking what you want from your known position, the thought experiment asks what rules might be chosen if the chooser did not know which position in society they would eventually occupy.

The larger political philosophy can be left aside here. The epistemic operation is enough: some information about the rule-maker's particular interests is deliberately removed.

Abstract it.

The structure changes from:

my known position→social rule\text{my known position} \rightarrow \text{social rule}my known position→social rule

toward:

reduced knowledge of my position→social conjecture.\text{reduced knowledge of my position} \rightarrow \text{social conjecture}.reduced knowledge of my position→social conjecture.

That weakens one route by which the self can be projected into the rule. It does not create certainty. The result remains an abstraction, another tentative solution that can be criticized.

The important move is therefore more general than the thought experiment itself. A moral abstraction can improve when an assumption smuggled into the rule becomes visible and can be removed or revised. That is the same operation performed when any compressed interface fails: the compact rule is decompressed until the hidden assumption becomes available to criticism.

Inherited ethical traditions can be understood in the same way. Christianity contains moral explanations. So do Judaism, Islam, Buddhism, classical philosophy, Enlightenment liberalism, indigenous traditions, secular humanism, and other bodies of inherited knowledge. They contain conjectures about forgiveness, charity, obligation, dignity, cruelty, sacrifice, strangers, family, responsibility, mercy, and justice.

Some of those conjectures may be profound. Some may have contributed to institutions that later became secular. Some may survive criticism; others may not. Some may acquire new meaning after being separated from the metaphysical structure in which they first appeared.

None of that establishes:

contains valuable ethical knowledge→should govern society as an authority.\text{contains valuable ethical knowledge} \rightarrow \text{should govern society as an authority}.contains valuable ethical knowledge→should govern society as an authority.

A liberal society does not need to become a Christian nation in order to inherit ethical knowledge that developed inside Christianity. Nor does the historical origin of an ethical conjecture immunize it from criticism.

Objective knowledge can outlive allegiance to its source. A conjecture can leave the tradition that produced it, be criticized by outsiders, be recombined with other conjectures, enter law or ordinary morality, and later be rejected. What survives is not the authority of the source but whatever explanatory knowledge survives criticism.

The danger grows when a compressed moral abstraction acquires institutional reach. At the interpersonal scale, the move can be:

kindness means X, therefore I should do X to you.

At larger scale:

a good life requires X, therefore society should require X.

The causal path is:

ethical conjecture→cultural compression→norm→institution→scaled consequence.\text{ethical conjecture} \rightarrow \text{cultural compression} \rightarrow \text{norm} \rightarrow \text{institution} \rightarrow \text{scaled consequence}.ethical conjecture→cultural compression→norm→institution→scaled consequence.

The initial conjecture may have solved a real problem. The norm may have coordinated many people. But scaling preserves error as readily as it preserves knowledge.

That is why phrases such as decent people do this, this is respectful, this is what families are for, this is what children need, and this is normal can become epistemically dangerous. They may compress good explanations. They may also conceal the absence of one.

Criticism requires the compression to be opened again. What problem was the value solving? What assumptions about persons does it contain? Whose problem world does it fit? What happens when it is applied to someone whose epistemic landscape differs? What would make the value change?

Abstract society cannot avoid norms. The epistemic question is whether they remain criticizable. That question now turns inward.

5. The Inward Turn

The problem of projection does not stop at the boundary between you and someone else. You also direct compressed moral abstractions toward yourself.

Be kind to yourself. Respect yourself. Listen to yourself. Forgive yourself. Give yourself what you need.

These phrases may be valuable, but their compactness hides the same problem that appeared between persons. Which self? Which problem? Which retained tentative solution? Which inexplicit expectation? Which fear? Which bodily signal? Which future consequence?

Your explicit surface does not possess transparent access to the whole epistemic mind beneath it. You contain explicit explanations, inexplicit skills, habits, memories, emotions, expectations, inherited norms, unfinished problems, conflicting interests, bodily constraints, forgotten experiences, current values, and emerging contradictions.

So the explicit narrator cannot simply announce:

this is what is kind to me.

That conclusion is itself a conjecture.

Suppose you feel exhausted. Rest may be kind. But perhaps the exhaustion is partly produced by avoidance of a problem that becomes worse when avoided. Suppose you are anxious about a social situation. Avoidance may reduce distress now. But perhaps repeated avoidance preserves the explanation generating the fear. Suppose you are working relentlessly. Stopping may relieve strain. But perhaps the work is the most alive problem in your life, and stopping would create a different form of suffering.

The point is not that rest, avoidance, or stopping are wrong. It is that no universal behavioral translation follows from the compressed value alone. Be kind to yourself is a compressed value. To become explanatory, it has to be decompressed into a problem situation. The relevant knowledge may not yet exist.

That changes the ordinary picture of introspection. You do not always look inward and discover a completed answer waiting to be found. Sometimes the answer becomes available only after an attempted change produces consequences that can be criticized.

You change a routine. Speak to someone. Stop speaking to someone. Work less. Work more. Set a boundary. Remove one. Return to an old problem. Revise an expectation. Question a value. Attempt a feared activity. Abandon an inherited obligation. Reconstruct a relationship.

The important relation is not the list of actions. It is what happens next. The consequence becomes new information. The attempt can be criticized. The problem changes.

Self-knowledge is therefore created through error correction. The attempt does not merely reveal a fixed self. It can change the problem situation from which the next explanation must be made.

There is a constraint on this process: errors differ in reversibility, and some actions damage the conditions for further correction. That changes the practical structure of experimentation. Recoverable errors leave a larger error-correction surface; difficult-to-reverse actions leave less room for criticism to alter what happens next. The epistemic structure remains the same even when the permissible range of trial changes.

Nor does successful self-criticism end in a permanently harmonious person. Your internal abstractions can conflict. A value can oppose an interest. A fear can conflict with a desire. A social role can conflict with a private problem. A new explanation can make an old commitment unstable.

Sometimes you create an explanation that reorganizes several tensions at once. A previously mysterious feeling becomes intelligible. An avoidance pattern becomes criticizable. Two values that appeared incompatible are reframed. A problem that dominated attention loses its force.

Abstract it.

The result can feel like resolution, but the stronger description is temporary consilience:

conflicting structures→new explanation→reduced contradiction→new action space.\text{conflicting structures} \rightarrow \text{new explanation} \rightarrow \text{reduced contradiction} \rightarrow \text{new action space}.conflicting structures→new explanation→reduced contradiction→new action space.

The new explanation brings several parts of the internal landscape into temporary coherence. Later another problem appears and the equilibrium breaks. That is not a failure of the earlier explanation merely because the harmony was temporary. It is what an open epistemic life is expected to look like.

The inward turn therefore changes the social argument. If even your own compressed values may need to be decompressed before their meaning becomes clear, then an institution's compressed representation of another person has an even weaker claim to that person.

The question is not whether abstraction is avoidable. It is what happens when abstraction meets reality again.

6. Reality, Reach, and Governability

Increasing abstraction does not mean that society has escaped from reality. People sit behind screens, trade symbols, manage institutions, manipulate models, work with money rather than material, interact through text, use artificial intelligence, and inhabit abstractions built on earlier abstractions.

None of that makes the consequences unreal. A bank account can be abstract and determine whether someone can buy food. A legal order can be abstract and determine whether someone is imprisoned. A software bug can be abstract and stop a physical system. A financial instrument can be abstract and reorganize physical production. An idea can be abstract and help cause a war.

So the relevant contrast is not:

abstractvs.real.\text{abstract} \quad \text{vs.} \quad \text{real}.abstractvs.real.

The useful contrast is between abstractions that survive criticism in the transformations for which they are used and abstractions that do not. In compressed form:

good abstractionvs.bad abstraction.\text{good abstraction} \quad \text{vs.} \quad \text{bad abstraction}.good abstractionvs.bad abstraction.

More precisely:

Which abstraction better explains and enables the relevant transformation, and which errors become visible when it is used?

Reality remains the constraint. Abstraction is increasingly how you act within it.

That can enlarge your reach. Writing lets you think with the dead. Mathematics lets you manipulate relationships far beyond intuitive perception. Law can let strangers coordinate without personal dominance. Software can let one person instantiate knowledge at enormous scale. Scientific theories let you act on structures you cannot directly perceive. Artificial intelligence can let you interrogate objective knowledge across domains you could not previously enter.

You remain finite while the reachable problem situation expands. That matters because your internal epistemic landscape is already vast. The external abstract world can become an extension of your explanatory reach. You can enter a new field, translate a technical paper, interrogate a law, explore an unfamiliar culture, reconstruct a historical argument, model a system, compare explanations, or use artificial intelligence to reorganize objective knowledge around a problem that matters only to you.

The abstract society can therefore enlarge individuals without making them less individual.

But the same architecture can reverse direction. A credit model can classify you. An algorithm can decide what information becomes visible to you. A platform can shape salience. A bureaucratic category can determine eligibility. A workplace system can reduce you to measurable outputs. A social norm can convert dissent into defect. An artificial intelligence system can make you legible to an institution more quickly than you can understand the institution.

So abstraction can produce both:

abstraction→expanded reach\text{abstraction} \rightarrow \text{expanded reach}abstraction→expanded reach

and:

abstraction→expanded governability.\text{abstraction} \rightarrow \text{expanded governability}.abstraction→expanded governability.

There is no contradiction. The same compression that lets you navigate complexity can let an institution navigate you.

This returns us to the central asymmetry. You gain access to transformations because an abstraction hides complexity from you. An institution can gain power over you for the same reason: the abstraction can hide from you the assumptions, categories, and selection procedures through which you are being represented.

The issue is therefore not whether society uses abstractions. It must. The issue is whether an abstraction can become visible as an abstraction when it becomes problematic. That is the point at which dependence either remains criticizable or becomes epistemic submission.

7. The Empirical Objection Returns

We can now return to the problem: If individual epistemic minds are so vast, why does society work?

Society does not generally require mutual understanding. It requires sufficiently compatible interfaces.

If abstract society is so complex, why does ordinary life often feel simple?

Successful abstraction hides much of the complexity it organizes.

If people inhabit deeply different worlds, why can they share norms?

Norms compress coordination knowledge into interfaces that can function despite different reconstructions.

If those norms work, why criticize them?

Successful compression does not make the assumptions inside it infallible.

If society increasingly depends on abstraction, why does that not detach us from reality?

Abstractions are real causal structures acting inside one reality.

If abstraction frees you, why worry about dependence?

The same lower reconstruction burden that expands your action can also create opacity, exclusion, scalable error, and governability.

The empirical tension therefore does not refute the thesis. It sharpens it. The surprising efficiency of social coordination may itself be evidence of how much epistemic complexity objective abstractions successfully conceal. And how much work your epistemic mind is performing.

That explanation must remain criticizable. If the abstract-society thesis is right, several broad patterns should be expected.

  • As social systems become more complex, more coordination should occur through interfaces that reduce the need for participants to reconstruct underlying causal structures.

  • As abstraction layers accumulate, people should become capable of causing transformations whose implementation they do not explicitly understand.

  • Differences in the ability to navigate relevant abstractions should increasingly affect practical access to institutions, technologies, and opportunities.

  • Generational differences should sometimes appear not merely as different preferences but as differences in access to distinct regions of objective abstract knowledge.

  • Social norms should sometimes function effectively while concealing incompatible interpretations among the people following them. When those interfaces break down, hidden assumptions should become easier to see.

  • Institutional errors should sometimes persist because the abstraction has become difficult to decompress into the problem it was originally solving.

  • Technologies that help people reconstruct objective knowledge should expand the range of abstractions they can criticize.

These are observations. They are points at which the explanation can encounter error and criticism.

The stronger thesis would weaken if increasingly detailed investigation instead showed that successful coordination usually depends on deep convergence of internal mental structures, that abstraction layers do not materially reduce reconstruction burdens, or that social complexity does not increasingly depend on nested objective abstractions.

The abstract society is therefore not offered as a metaphor for modern life, or as a replacement for the metaphor of the social mind. It is an explanation with consequences: increasing coordination through compressed interfaces, increasing action without reconstruction, and increasing dependence on structures whose assumptions become visible most clearly when they fail.

That returns the argument to you, the person who must live inside those structures.

8. Dependence Without Epistemic Submission

The scale of abstract society can make you feel invisible. Markets, states, religions, corporations, platforms, cultures, institutions, networks, models, and artificial intelligence can all appear larger than any individual. They can exert causal power beyond any one person and persist across generations.

At this scale, talk of a social mind or social knowledge can capture something real. Objective knowledge persists across generations. Networks distribute and transform it. Institutions select and instantiate it. Interactions among these structures can produce emergent effects that belong to the larger system rather than to any individual participant.

But the compression has to be reopened before it can do epistemic work. Which knowledge is objective and persistent? Which transformations arise from the network? Which effects appear only at scale? And where are new explanations created when the inherited structure becomes problematic?

The first three questions may lead naturally to descriptions of collective or emergent organization. The last returns us to you and your epistemic mind. The capacity to encounter a problem, create an explanation, understand criticism, and alter the problem situation remains, so far, located there.

That does not deny emergence. It identifies what emergence has not yet explained. The abstractions exist because people created, inherited, instantiated, interpreted, criticized, and reproduced them. Their future changes when people make them problematic again. A law becomes a problem. A norm becomes a problem. A market arrangement becomes a problem. A software interface becomes a problem. A moral value becomes a problem. Your own self-conception becomes a problem. Then a tentative solution appears.

You cannot reconstruct everything, and finite participation does not require you to. Abstraction is what makes participation possible. You can depend on knowledge you do not possess explicitly.

That dependence is not itself epistemic submission. The distinction turns on what the abstraction does to the problem situation available to you. An interface determines what becomes visible and which actions become easy. A category determines what can be entered. An institution determines which representations count. An algorithm determines which possibilities are presented. A norm determines which behavior appears natural.

You may therefore be free to act while acting only inside a problem situation someone else compressed.

The question becomes more precise:

How can you depend on abstractions without becoming epistemically subordinate to them?

Complete reconstruction cannot be the answer. No finite person can reconstruct civilization.

The alternative is not to eliminate dependence but to preserve some route by which dependence can become a problem. An abstraction can be reopened when it fails. Its original problem can be recovered. Its assumptions can be exposed. Alternatives can be compared. Exclusions can become visible. The interface can be revised, the value rejected, the institution changed, or a new abstraction created.

That is epistemic autonomy in the abstract society. It is not knowing everything. It is remaining capable, when necessary, of making the abstraction itself available to criticism, and to follow that criticism wherever your problem situation leads you.

The abstract society is not a departure from the epistemic individual. It is what happens when billions of epistemic minds leave traces of their knowledge in a common world. Those traces accumulate into institutions, technologies, norms, values, and interfaces. They expand what later people can do. They also shape what later people can see.

Society becomes more abstract while you remain finite. The deepest freedom available to you is therefore not an escape from abstraction. It is the continued possibility of recognizing an abstraction as an abstraction, making it a problem, and creating what comes next.

Artificial intelligence may become the interface through which you encounter much of the objective knowledge civilization has created. It can help you reconstruct explanations, but it can also select, filter, and frame which problems become visible.

You cannot reconstruct everything behind that interface. Yet your autonomy depends upon being able to question it.

What does it mean to understand an abstraction well enough to criticize it, when complete reconstruction is impossible?
