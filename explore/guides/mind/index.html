Mind

You may come to Inepistemic from cognitive science, neuroscience, philosophy of mind, consciousness research, psychology, agency, creativity, computation, or another account of how minds work.

This book does not attempt navigate those accounts, and this guide does not assume that the questions it raises are unfamiliar within those fields. Its narrower purpose is to make visible a set of epistemic distinctions that may be useful when the object of interest is a mind: distinctions concerning problems, criticism, inexplicit knowledge, explanation, abstraction, future knowledge, external artifacts, and the singular history through which a person changes.

The manuscript's unusual move is to take a universal epistemology of knowledge creation and direct it inward, toward the singular person in whom knowing occurs. That produces a recurring question:

What changes when a mind is described not only by its present states or mechanisms, but also by the problems, criticisms, reconstructions, and explanatory transitions through which its knowledge changes?

Readers in the sciences and philosophy of mind have much richer mechanistic, computational, dynamical, phenomenological, developmental, and biological accounts of the phenomena encountered here. The manuscript does not ask those accounts to yield to its vocabulary. It asks what becomes clearer when several specifically universal epistemic relations are kept distinct.

Begin with the explanatory object

Start with 00 - Universality.

The opening chapter treats the same singular person as constrained by physical, computational, evolutionary, and epistemological explanations without assuming that any one vocabulary exhausts the person. For this reading path, the important move is methodological: the singular person becomes an explanatory object in their own right.

That distinction becomes more concrete in 01 - Genetics and 02 - Populations. Population regularities, measured traits, classifications, and associations can all contain genuine knowledge. The manuscript's narrower point is that knowledge about variation across people and an explanation of the causal history of one person are different explanatory objects.

For readers interested in mind, the useful distinction is therefore not population knowledge versus individual knowledge as competing forms of knowledge. It is:

between-person structure / within-person causal and epistemic history.

A representation may be informative at one level without containing the relations needed at the other.

Present state / open epistemic future

05 - Openness introduces one of the manuscript's central distinctions: present knowledge / future explanatory knowledge.

The chapter does not require the claim that the future is physically indeterminate. Its epistemic claim is narrower. If an explanation is genuinely new knowledge, then it is not already possessed as that explanation in the present state of the knower. A current description may constrain, predict, or model many aspects of a person without thereby already containing the future explanation that person may later create.

This distinction becomes important whenever a description of a mind is asked to do more than describe or predict its current organization. The manuscript keeps separate:

  • what is presently represented;
  • what can presently be predicted;
  • what explanatory knowledge has not yet been created.

The same chapter treats conscious availability as another possible epistemic distinction. The manuscript does not offer a theory of consciousness. It asks whether becoming consciously available can alter what knowledge, expectation, or conflict can enter explicit criticism and reconstruction.

For readers already working with distinctions among access, reportability, attention, metacognition, or conscious processing, the interest here is specifically epistemic: what changes when some content becomes available to criticism?

Abstraction / experienced reality

06 - Abstractions follows the consequences of accumulated knowledge.

The manuscript treats abstractions as indispensable. They compress causal and explanatory structure so that finite people can think and act without reconstructing everything beneath the representation. But an abstraction can become so familiar that its status as an abstraction disappears from experience.

The useful distinction here is:

usable representation / causal structure represented.

The chapter is interested in what happens when that distinction becomes difficult to recover. Prior knowledge can then shape expectation, salience, interpretation, and later problem formation while no longer appearing to the person as an interpretation at all.

This is not offered as a replacement for psychological or neural accounts of categorization, priors, attention, perception, or learning. It is an epistemic description of what is at stake when a representation becomes functionally transparent to the person using it.

Problem availability / problem activation

09 - Problems sharpens a distinction that recurs throughout the book.

A problem can be objectively available — represented in a text, visible to another person, implicit in contradictory claims, or encoded in a task — without becoming an active problem for a particular person.

The manuscript therefore distinguishes:

an available problem / a problem reconstructed within a person's problem situation.

This is where individual history matters. Retained knowledge changes which distinctions are available; those distinctions change what conflicts become salient; those conflicts can become new problems; solving them changes the conditions from which later problems emerge.

The manuscript calls this recursive structure problem-world formation. For a reader interested in mind, its value is not that minds have histories — that is already familiar across many traditions — but that the history is described specifically in terms of which problems can become epistemically active next.

Error / criticism

Read 08 - Criticism alongside 09 - Problems.

The manuscript separates the detection of failure from the creation of a criticism.

An error, conflict, failed prediction, or unsatisfactory outcome does not by itself specify:

  • which assumption should be abandoned;
  • which relation was misunderstood;
  • which abstraction should change;
  • which criterion should be revised;
  • or what successor problem should replace the earlier one.

The relevant distinction is therefore:

error detection / explanatory criticism.

Criticism can itself require conjecture. A system may register inconsistency without yet possessing an explanation of what the inconsistency means.

This is also why the chapter distinguishes an objective carrier of a conflict from the reconstruction through which that conflict becomes a live problem for a particular epistemic subject. A text, model, machine, conversation, or memory can preserve incompatible structures. The epistemic transition occurs when the conflict is reconstructed in a way that changes the person's problem situation.

Inexplicit knowledge / explicit representation

13 - Inexplicit is central to this reading path.

The manuscript allows knowledge to participate in action, expectation, recognition, judgment, skill, salience, and interpretation without first existing as an explicit proposition available for report.

It then makes a further distinction:

explicitation / transcription.

To make something explicit need not be to retrieve a fully formed hidden sentence. The explicit representation may itself be newly created. It can be recognized, modified, rejected, compared, or criticized, and the process of making it explicit can therefore change the problem.

For readers interested in introspection, metacognition, tacit knowledge, implicit processing, or report, the manuscript's contribution is not to settle how those capacities are implemented. It is to preserve the epistemic difference between:

knowledge that participates in cognition / the explicit object later created to represent that knowledge.

That explicit object is then fallible in its own right.

Explanation possessed / explanation allowed to reach you

14 - Seriously introduces another distinction that matters when the subject and object of explanation can coincide.

A person can possess an explanation without permitting its consequences to reorganize the rest of their problem situation. The chapter calls attention to reach: an explanation becomes epistemically consequential when its implications are allowed to create new conflicts, criticisms, and problems elsewhere in what the person thinks they know.

For mind-related inquiry, this creates a recursive case. An explanation of cognition can itself become part of the cognitive environment of the person who understands it. Self-description is therefore not necessarily passive representation. Once reconstructed, it can alter expectations, attention, available criticism, or subsequent inquiry.

The distinction is:

having an explanation / allowing the explanation to generate successor problems.

Class capacity / singular self-explaining person

15 - Personhood turns a class-level claim into a person-level one.

The manuscript begins from the idea of a universal explainer and then asks what changes when that capacity is instantiated in one singular person who can direct explanation back toward themselves.

The epistemic movement is:

class→instance→self-explanationclass \rightarrow instance \rightarrow \text{self-explanation}class→instance→self-explanation

The chapter does not propose that self-explanation becomes complete. The opposite is more important. Each explanation of yourself can become objective knowledge that returns to your own problem situation and becomes material for further criticism.

This produces the distinction:

self-description / epistemic completion.

A person may become increasingly explicit to themselves without arriving at a final inventory that closes the possibility of further explanation.

Sudden appearance / causal discontinuity

16 - Mystery is useful for readers interested in creativity, insight, conscious access, and apparently discontinuous change.

The manuscript distinguishes the appearance of a sudden transition from a claim about its causal origin. A thought, interpretation, criterion, or creative solution can appear abruptly at the level available to report while still depending on processes whose relevant causal structure is not presently explained.

The chapter therefore treats mystery as an epistemic relation to missing explanation rather than as evidence, by itself, of a break in causality.

A second distinction concerns evaluation. The criteria under which a tentative solution is judged need not remain fixed throughout an inquiry. Criticism can alter the problem and the criteria together.

For creativity-related readers, the useful separation is:

search within a represented problem / transformation of the problem and its evaluative criteria.

The manuscript leaves open what mechanisms implement such transformations. Its concern is to keep the two explanatory objects distinct.

State / transition

17 - Arrows makes the relations between epistemic states into explicit explanatory objects.

A representation can identify successive states — a problem, a tentative solution, a criticism, a successor problem — without explaining the transformation between them.

The central distinction is:

state description / transition explanation.

This matters for any account that can observe, represent, or classify successive cognitive states. The manuscript's claim is not that such accounts ignore transition. It is that, within its own epistemic model, the arrows cannot be treated as empty connectors. The transition may contain the creation of a distinction, the change of a criterion, the activation of a criticism, or the reconstruction of a problem.

The chapter also separates temporal succession from epistemic direction. The next event in time is not necessarily the transition that reorganizes what can be thought next.

Prediction / future explanation

18 - Unpredictability returns to the open-future distinction with greater precision.

The chapter is easiest to misread if three different claims are collapsed:

  1. a future explanation is not presently known;
  2. a genuinely new explanation is not already present as that explanation in current knowledge;
  3. the physical future is indeterminate.

The manuscript's argument concerns the first two. It does not need the third.

Its epistemic distinction is therefore:

model of the present person / possession of that person's future explanatory knowledge.

A model may remain highly informative while not containing knowledge that has not yet been created. The chapter's concern is the explanatory overreach that occurs when those two achievements are treated as equivalent.

Model of another person / continuing person

25 - Taboo extends the same distinction to other minds.

A representation of another person can be detailed, predictive, causally informative, emotionally important, and still remain a representation of someone who may continue creating knowledge beyond it.

The useful distinction is:

reconstruction of another person / substitution of the reconstruction for the person.

The chapter develops this through love, projection, death, memory, and surviving objective traces. Its epistemic concern is what a model cannot preserve when the modeled person is itself a continuing source of future explanations.

This is not a claim that other-person modeling is uniquely defective. It is a reminder that the object being modeled may itself continue changing the explanatory space.

What this guide is claiming

This reading path does not ask a neuroscientist, cognitive scientist, psychologist, philosopher, or consciousness researcher to accept that their field has neglected these phenomena. Nor does it offer an alternative mechanism where those fields already have competing mechanistic accounts.

Its narrower proposal is that several epistemic distinctions recur when a singular knowledge-creating person is treated as the object of explanation. Those distinctions may then be carried into whatever account of mind the reader already finds productive, where they can be criticized, mapped onto mechanisms, rejected, or refined.

The manuscript's question for this audience is therefore:

Which epistemic relations become easier to see when problems, criticism, explanation, inexplicit knowledge, externalization, and future knowledge are kept distinct while explaining a mind?
