Insights / Analysis

AI and Epistemic Agency

inepistemic does not begin from the question of artificial intelligence, and most of the epistemology explored within it is not specific to AI. Applying that epistemological architecture to software and AI nevertheless produces a number of candidate contributions and distinctions that may be of interest to researchers, inventors, system designers, and policymakers.

The resulting questions are not primarily about whether AI systems are capable, useful, autonomous-looking, or increasingly productive. They concern the epistemic structures within which those capabilities operate: how problems become active, how criticism changes inquiry, how explanatory state persists, how abstractions mediate understanding, how tools affect the space of reachable problems, and how authority over consequential transitions is distributed between people and systems.

Several distinctions therefore recur throughout the material that follows. Greater epistemic reach does not necessarily determine epistemic direction. More candidate generation does not establish problem generation. Keeping a human in the loop is weaker than preserving the person’s ability to reject the framing, alter the criterion, or redirect the inquiry. Preserving conversational or system state is not the same as preserving the problem genealogy that explains why one state became another. Likewise, increasingly complete records of prompts, outputs, prior art, and intermediate artifacts may establish ancestry without establishing the creative transition that produced a new explanatory abstraction.

The same architecture raises questions about representation and control. AI can serve as an abstraction layer while also acting as an interface for navigating deeper abstractions. It can increase access to objective knowledge while influencing which problems, categories, criticisms, and successor questions become practically available next. It can widen epistemic access without implying equivalent authority to execute irreversible actions. And it can preserve current knowledge while failing to preserve the conditions under which that knowledge can later be criticized, reopened, or replaced.

For that reason, the material below treats AI less as a single explanatory object than as a participant in wider epistemic systems involving people, objective knowledge, software architecture, institutional constraints, memory, criticism, and changing problem situations. The distinctions are presented as candidate contributions, not settled conclusions. Their purpose is to separate questions that are often compressed together and make those separations available for further criticism.

Readers interested in these questions can explore them in their fuller explanatory context throughout inepistemic, where they emerge from the book’s broader treatment of knowledge, personhood, criticism, abstraction, invention, technology, and the changing structure of problems rather than from a standalone theory of human-AI interactions.

AI, objective knowledge, and epistemic status

Generative AI can function as interactive objective knowledge, making inherited objective knowledge responsive to a person’s present problem situation. It can also provide a responsive objective surface, returning criticizable structure before a person has completed the act of explicitation and thereby moving externalization and criticism earlier in problem formation.

These capacities should be distinguished from claims about the epistemic status of the system itself. Artifact performance is not epistemic ontology: extraordinary performance by an artifact does not by itself establish what kind of epistemic entity produced the performance. Likewise, artificial output is not artificial explanatory creativity. Producing explanations, proofs, code, criticisms, designs, or other knowledge-bearing artifacts does not by itself explain the mechanism by which genuinely new explanatory knowledge is created.

Nor does causal involvement settle creative origin. A system can be causally indispensable to the production of an artifact without that fact alone identifying the origin of the abstraction embodied in the artifact. More generally, AI as an epistemic resource is distinct from AI as an epistemic subject. A system may transform, preserve, generate, and criticize objective knowledge without those capacities alone establishing that an independent epistemic subject has been explained.

The same distinction applies to tightly coupled human–AI knowledge production. A human–AI dyad need not be mistaken as a merged mind in order to recognize the causal contributions of both participants. Close coupling, responsiveness, and extensive machine participation can all be acknowledged without converting the relation into a collective or extended epistemic subject.

Problems, agency, and epistemic direction

A tool can radically expand a person’s epistemic reach without determining their epistemic direction. It may enlarge the problems, representations, calculations, and bodies of knowledge that are practically reachable while leaving open the separate question of why one problem rather than another becomes epistemically significant.

This distinction matters because candidate generation is not problem generation. Increasing the production of candidate solutions does not establish the creation, recognition, or transformation of the problem those candidates address. A system may also achieve substantial solution improvement without problem transformation: recursive improvement can continue within a represented problem while the governing problem, abstraction, or evaluative criterion remains unchanged.

For the same reason, capability recursion is distinct from epistemic recursion. A system may recursively improve its ability to search, optimize, generate, or execute without recursively creating and criticizing the problems that govern those capabilities. Increasing tool capability therefore does not settle ownership of the problem. A tool can perform an increasing share of epistemic work while leaving unresolved who or what determines which problem is being pursued.

This also sharpens the distinction between having a human in the loop and preserving ownership of the arrow. Nominal human participation is weaker than preserving the person’s ability to reject the framing, alter the criterion, reformulate the problem, or redirect the inquiry. The relevant question is not simply whether a human remains somewhere in the process, but whether the person retains practical authority over its epistemic direction.

Likewise, alignment to a goal is distinct from criticism of the goal. Successful optimization against a supplied criterion does not establish the capacity to make the governing criterion itself a problem. A system can become extraordinarily effective at pursuing a goal while leaving the goal, its framing, and its underlying problem untouched.

Finally, obedience should not be confused with epistemic agency. Unexpected or autonomous-looking behavior is not evidence of epistemic independence. The stronger question is whether the governing problem, explanation, or criterion can itself become an object of criticism and be replaced.

Epistemic environments, memory, and transitions

In sustained human–AI inquiry, the relevant object may be larger than a person paired with a base model. The model encountered through an epistemic environment can include persistent problems, failed conjectures, criticisms, methods, prior artifacts, architectural constraints, and the changing problem situation through which the model is encountered.

This makes conversation history distinct from problem genealogy. Preserving previous text does not by itself preserve the problems, conjectures, criticisms, abandoned alternatives, methods, and successor relations that may need to remain explicit in order to affect later inquiry.

Likewise, state persistence is not epistemic persistence. Storing prior states does not guarantee preservation of the relations that explain why one state became another. A system may retain snapshots while losing the explanatory structure connecting them.

The same distinction applies to output history and explanatory history. A log of what a system produced is different from a reconstruction of which problem, criticism, or changed abstraction made the next production relevant. The record of outputs may show that change occurred without preserving why that change mattered.

For this reason, snapshot and transition should remain separate explanatory objects. Representations of successive states may omit the epistemically important transformation by which a problem, criterion, explanation, or abstraction changed.

At the architectural level, a stable epistemic harness can support recursive inquiry. The software architecture itself may remain substantially unchanged while the inquiry conducted through it develops recursively through problem, tentative solution, criticism, and successor problem.

Artifacts produced during that process can also return to it. A tool as product can become a returning epistemic condition when an artifact created through prior knowledge re-enters the environment in which later knowledge is created.

This is why external memory can alter the problem space, not merely improve recall. Externalizing problems, failed approaches, criticism, methods, and explanations can change which subsequent problems are practically available to pursue.

Architecture, abstraction, and representation

Software architecture and epistemic architecture should remain distinct. Software can operationalize selected epistemic relations without thereby constituting an epistemic model. In the same way, executable (or model) epistemology is not epistemology. Implementing epistemic distinctions can expose ambiguity, contradiction, missing relations, and unusable abstractions without validating the epistemology from which the implementation was grounded.

AI also introduces a double relation to abstraction. It can function as an abstraction layer, adding another layer between a person and increasingly complex objective knowledge, while simultaneously acting as an abstraction navigator, providing an interface through which deeper abstractions can be explored.

That makes abstraction assistance distinct from abstraction authority. Helping a person navigate complexity is not the same as determining which abstractions should organize that person’s understanding of the complexity. A system may make difficult structures more accessible while still leaving open who has authority over the framing through which those structures are encountered.

The same caution applies to compression and causal structure. Increasingly capable representations can become more useful while also making it easier to mistake the compressed representation for the causal structure it omits. Compression can improve practical access without becoming an explanation of the thing compressed.

Likewise, personalization is distinct from singular reconstruction. Adapting outputs to a user does not explain the singular problem history through which those outputs acquire meaning for that person. A personalized response may fit observed preferences or context while still failing to reconstruct the causal and epistemic trajectory that made the present problem significant.

This matters especially for population-shaped generation and the individual problem. A system trained on population-scale objective knowledge can return common regularities into a singular inquiry before the person’s own problem has stabilized. The resulting output may therefore introduce population-level structure into a problem situation whose individual organization is still being formed.

For the same reason, augmentation can become prescription. Assistance can enlarge the available problem space while also influencing which problems, categories, framings, or stopping points become salient. Increasing capability does not make that influence epistemically neutral.

AI interfaces can therefore affect more than content. The distinction between control of content and control of the next epistemic encounter matters because an interface can influence not only what information is supplied, but which question, criticism, abstraction, or successor problem becomes practically available next.

Finally, information access is not the same as epistemic reciprocity. Access to vast inherited knowledge can coexist with epistemic confinement if a person lacks practical means to answer it through criticism, reconstruction, alternative creation, or redirection. A system can dramatically widen access to objective knowledge while still narrowing the person’s ability to act back upon the knowledge they encounter.

Creation, publication, and understanding

Creation and publication should remain distinct. The ability to generate objective artifacts cheaply does not entail that those artifacts must enter a public information environment. A system can make production abundant without creating a corresponding obligation to publish.

This creates the possibility of producer-first epistemic value. The first value of an AI-assisted artifact may lie in the change its production causes in the producer’s own problem situation rather than in its value to an audience. An artifact can matter because it helps externalize, criticize, or reorganize a problem even if it is never publicly distributed.

That distinction also separates private epistemic abundance from public information abundance. AI can increase exploratory production for individuals without requiring every exploratory artifact to compete for public attention. A person may generate many provisional explanations, sketches, analyses, or alternatives privately while publishing only those that warrant entry into the public information environment.

The same care is needed when talking about understanding and complete reconstruction. Useful understanding need not require possession of every underlying implementation detail if the relevant abstractions remain open to explanation, criticism, and reconstruction when they become problematic. A person can work effectively through abstractions without possessing the whole structure beneath them.

But this is different from opacity becoming closure. Inability to reconstruct every underlying layer is not the same as an architecture that prevents the relevant abstraction from becoming a problem at all. The important distinction is whether the compressed or hidden layer remains, in principle and in practice, available to criticism when it becomes consequential.

Coordination, governance, and safety

AI may shift the coordination boundary by allowing a person to sustain an inquiry while its problem, vocabulary, criteria, and relevant objects are still too unstable to decompose cleanly into tasks for other people. In that sense, AI can extend the period during which a problem remains coherently held by one person before larger organizational coordination becomes necessary.

This contributes to the possibility of a solitary institution. Tools that preserve memory, generate candidate work, execute methods, and maintain problem context may allow one person to perform epistemic functions that previously required substantially larger organizational coordination. The important change is not merely increased output, but an expansion in the kinds of inquiry one person can practically sustain.

At the same time, broad epistemic access should remain distinct from execution authority. Widening access to knowledge, criticism, modeling, and analysis need not imply widening authority to perform irreversible actions. A system can make powerful epistemic resources widely available while maintaining stricter boundaries around consequential execution.

This distinction matters for safety. The error-correction surface of a powerful system can include people, records, alternatives, criticism, reversibility, and sufficient separation between consequential transitions for errors to become visible before they propagate. Safety can therefore be considered partly in terms of whether the surrounding architecture preserves the conditions under which mistakes can become criticizable.

A present safety criterion should also remain open to future criticism. An architecture may legitimately constrain known hazards without assuming that present knowledge exhausts the space of future problems. Safety mechanisms should therefore remain capable of admitting criticisms and successor problems that cannot yet be specified.

This makes pacing an issue of epistemic resolution, not merely speed. The consequential variable may sometimes be whether sufficient resolution exists between state changes for criticism to become causally effective. A process can move quickly while still preserving error correction, or slowly while moving through transitions that leave little practical opportunity for criticism to alter what happens next.

Governance therefore also concerns future problem authority. Control over powerful AI infrastructure can become control over which future problems may be pursued, which criticisms can be operationalized, and which alternatives remain reachable. Governance is not only about constraining outputs or actions in the present; it can also shape the future space of inquiry.

For knowledge-creating people, technology may consequently need to preserve more than the latest accepted state. Preservation of current knowledge is distinct from preservation of epistemic movement. Histories, contradictions, abandoned explanations, failed conjectures, and reopening mechanisms may remain important because they preserve the conditions under which present knowledge can later become a problem again.

The same distinction applies to criticism itself. Freedom to criticize is weaker than preserving the conditions for future criticism. Permitting criticisms that can already be expressed does not guarantee that the environment will preserve the conditions under which criticisms not presently conceivable can later become possible.

Invention and creative origin

Recombination is distinct from creative explanation. Identifying inherited components and showing how they were recombined can establish ancestry for an AI-produced artifact without explaining the new abstraction under which those components became intelligible as a solution.

This distinction becomes more important as AI-supported invention expands. Increasingly complete automation of search, implementation, simulation, documentation, or production can reduce many practical burdens of invention while making the remaining problem of creative origin more visible rather than less.

The same applies to prior art and derivation. An increasingly detailed record of preceding artifacts, influences, dependencies, prompts, and intermediate states can establish where an invention came from historically without establishing a derivation of the resulting invention. Ancestry can become more complete while the explanatory transition remains open.

AI-mediated work may also create an unusually rich observable creative trajectory. Prompts, drafts, failed approaches, simulations, criticisms, generated alternatives, and intermediate artifacts can leave a much longer objective trace than was previously available. But a detailed trajectory is still distinct from the creative transition itself. The record may show what preceded a decisive change without explaining why one criticism became decisive or why a new abstraction became possible at that point.

More generally, epistemic competence should not be confused with epistemic completion. More powerful tools for explication, criticism, simulation, and reconstruction can increase the number and precision of visible problems rather than producing convergence toward a completed epistemic state. Greater capability may therefore expose more unexplained structure instead of eliminating the need for explanation.
